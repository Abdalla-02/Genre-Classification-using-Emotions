### 2. "dissonant string cluster" — *meaning and alternative* (p. 7)

A **cluster** is a chord made of neighbouring notes played together (e.g. C, C♯, D), which
clashes; **dissonant** means it sounds tense and unresolved; **sustained** means it is held
for a long time. Together: the strings hold a tense, clashing chord. This is the typical
sound of horror scores, but the term is jargon. Alternative: "long, clashing chords in the
strings".

### 3. *What does that mean? Is there a simpler way to say the sentence?* (p. 7)

The marked sentence says: certain combinations of sound are typical of certain genres. A
held, tense string sound over a low beat suggests horror; brass playing a fanfare over
fast, heavy drums suggests action. Proposed (replaces the second and third sentence):

```latex
Long, clashing string chords over a low, steady beat suggest a horror film, and loud brass
over fast, heavy drums suggests an action film. A solo piano over soft strings suggests a
drama or a romance.
```

### 11. *What does that exactly mean? How is the highest limit calculated?* (p. 20, "listener panels")

**What it means.** The Eerola dataset has two sets of ratings. In the first, one group of
listeners (a *panel*) rated 360 excerpts. In the second, a different group rated 102 of
the same excerpts again. For those 102 excerpts there are therefore two independent
measurements of the same thing.

**How the limit is calculated.** For each emotion, the agreement between the two panels on
the 102 excerpts is measured with the intraclass correlation ICC(C,1):

  ICC(C,1) = (MS_excerpts − MS_error) / (MS_excerpts + MS_error)

MS_excerpts measures how much the excerpts differ from each other, and MS_error how much
the two panels disagree about the same excerpt (a two-way ANOVA with two raters). The
value is 1 if the panels agree perfectly and 0 if their ratings are unrelated. Averaged
over the eight emotions it is **0.897** (`experiments/features/exp4_rating_reliability.py`).

**Why it is a limit for R².** Every rating is the true emotion of the excerpt plus noise:
the opinions of a particular group of people. The ICC estimates the share of the rating
variance that is the true emotion. A model can at best predict that true part; it cannot
predict the noise. So the largest share of the ratings' variance any model can explain,
the largest R², is about 0.90. The best model reaches 0.558, which is 62 % of it. Proposed
replacement for the last three sentences of the R² paragraph:

```latex
One reference point is essential for interpreting it. The ratings are themselves noisy:
they reflect the judgement of one particular group of listeners. The Eerola dataset
allows this noise to be measured, because a second, independent group of listeners rated
102 of the clips again. The agreement between the two groups, measured per emotion by the
intraclass correlation and averaged over the eight emotions, is about 0.90. This value
estimates the share of the rating variance that reflects the music rather than the
particular listeners. No model can predict more than that share, so the attainable $R^2$
is about 0.90 and not 1.0 (Section~\ref{subsec:rating_reliability}).
```

### 13. *Add its formula and how it's calculated* (p. 22, Cohen's d)

Proposed replacement for the subsection's text (the formula is the one in
`experiments/genre/exp_emotion_genre.py`):

```latex
Several analyses do not evaluate a model but ask whether an emotion differs between genres,
for example whether fear is higher in Horror clips than in other clips. Cohen's $d$
expresses such a difference in units of the pooled standard deviation:
\begin{equation}
  d = \frac{\bar{x}_{g} - \bar{x}_{\neg g}}{s_p}, \qquad
  s_p = \sqrt{\frac{(n_{g}-1)\,s_{g}^2 + (n_{\neg g}-1)\,s_{\neg g}^2}{n_{g} + n_{\neg g} - 2}},
\end{equation}
where $\bar{x}_g$, $s_g$ and $n_g$ are the mean, standard deviation and number of the clips
that carry genre $g$, and $\bar{x}_{\neg g}$, $s_{\neg g}$ and $n_{\neg g}$ those of all
other clips. A positive $d$ means that the emotion is rated higher in the genre than
outside it. Because the difference is divided by the spread of the ratings, $d$ can be
compared across emotions that use their scales differently. By convention, $|d| \approx
0.2$ is a small effect, $0.5$ a medium and $0.8$ a large one; fear in Horror, for example,
reaches $d = +0.75$. The genre signatures quoted in Section~\ref{subsec:fund_why_not_va}
and reported in Section~\ref{sec:eval_signatures} are given in these units.
```

### 14. *Explain the whole paragraph in a different way* (p. 22, Statistical Significance)

The current paragraph mixes both tests in one block and still reads as if the
Nadeau–Bengio test were the main one. Since the switch to film level, the film bootstrap
is the main test, and the corrected t-test is the stricter check over clips. Proposed
replacement, one paragraph per test, with the formula and how it is computed (both match
the code: `exp_film_level.py`, `src/evaluation/repeated.py`):

```latex
Two models are often only a few hundredths of an $F_1$ point apart, and with 40 films such
a difference can arise by chance. Two tests are used to judge it, and they answer
different questions.

\paragraph{Paired bootstrap over films.} The first test asks whether a difference would
remain if the dataset had contained a different sample of films. A new set of test films
is drawn from the existing ones with replacement, so that some films occur twice and
others not at all. Both models are scored on this set, using the predictions they already
made, and the difference between them is recorded. Repeating this 2000 times gives a
distribution of the difference. Its central 95\,\% is the confidence interval, and the
two-sided $p$-value is twice the share of draws in which the difference is zero or has the
opposite sign. Whole films are drawn, never single clips, because the clips of a film are
not independent of each other. This is the test used for every film-level result.

\paragraph{Corrected paired $t$-test.} The second test asks whether the difference would
remain with other training data. Repeated cross-validation gives one score per test fold
for each model, fifty in all. An ordinary paired $t$-test on the fifty differences would
treat them as independent, but the folds share most of their training films, so it would
underestimate the variance and report differences as significant too readily.
\citet{nadeau2003inference} correct the variance for this overlap:
\begin{equation}
  t = \frac{\bar{d}}{\sqrt{\left(\frac{1}{J} + \frac{n_\text{test}}{n_\text{train}}\right)\hat{\sigma}^2}},
\end{equation}
where $\bar{d}$ and $\hat{\sigma}^2$ are the mean and variance of the $J = 50$ fold
differences and $n_\text{test}/n_\text{train} = 1/4$ for five folds; $t$ is compared with a
$t$-distribution with $J-1$ degrees of freedom. The added term does not shrink as more
repeats are run, which makes the test conservative and sets a lower limit on the $p$-value
it can reach on a given dataset (Appendix~\ref{subsec:statistical_power}). The test needs a
score for every test fold, and a test fold contains only about eight films, too few to
compute a per-genre $F_1$ over films. It is therefore applied to the clip-level scores, as
a stricter check beside the film bootstrap.

Both tests are reported where they apply, together with the number of the ten repeats in
which one model beats the other, which needs no assumption at all.
```

Note: the formula and the reference `subsec:statistical_power` already exist in the
thesis (Appendix A.1); `\text` needs `amsmath`, which the preamble loads.
