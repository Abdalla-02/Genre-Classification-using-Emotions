\chapter{Methods}
\label{ch:methods}

The method of this thesis maps a clip of film music to eight predicted emotion ratings. It then predicts the film's genres from these eight values alone (audio $\rightarrow$ 8 emotions $\rightarrow$ genre). The main experiments compare this two-stage pipeline with two alternatives. One gives the same audio representation directly to the same genre classifier. The other is a control that compresses the representation into eight dimensions without any link to emotion. Two datasets are used. The film-music dataset of \citet{eerola2011comparison} supplies the audio and the emotion ratings. The Blockbuster dataset of \citet{ma2021computational} supplies an independent set of films for testing. This chapter covers the data and their genre labels (Section~\ref{sec:methods_data}), the audio representations (Section~\ref{sec:methods_features}), the two models of the pipeline (Sections~\ref{sec:methods_stage1} and~\ref{sec:methods_stage2}), the baselines and controls (Section~\ref{sec:methods_baselines}), the evaluation protocol (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation_protocol}) and the implementation (Section~\ref{sec:methods_implementation}). Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} gives an overview. Chapter~\ref{ch:fundamentals} explains the underlying concepts; this chapter describes how they were applied.

\section{Datasets}
\label{sec:methods_data}

\subsection{The Eerola Film Soundtrack Dataset}
\label{subsec:methods_eerola}

The main dataset is the film-music set published by \citet{eerola2011comparison}. Its first set (Set~1) holds 360 clips of film music from 45 soundtracks. The clips are between 10 and 31\,s long (mean 17\,s). A panel of listeners rated each clip on the eight emotion scales of Section~\ref{sec:fund_emotion_def}, three dimensional and five discrete (Section~\ref{sec:fund_models}), each from 1 to 9. These are ratings of perceived emotion. The first stage of the pipeline learns the panel mean for each scale.

The second set (Set~2) holds 110 clips from Set~1, rated again by a different panel. Of these, 102 clips from 38 soundtracks can be matched clip by clip. On them the two panels agree with a mean correlation of $r=0.912$ and a consistency intraclass correlation, ICC(C,1), of 0.897. This value is taken as the ceiling on the $R^2$ an audio model can be expected to reach (Section~\ref{sec:eval_emotion}). Set~2 serves only to estimate how reliable the ratings are. It plays no part in training or evaluation.

The dataset has no genre information. Genre labels were added from the Internet Movie Database (IMDb), using the identifier of the film each soundtrack belongs to. The labels are the film's genre list, so all clips of a film carry the same labels. A gentle cue from an Action film is labelled Action. This is one reason why every split in this thesis is grouped by film (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation_protocol}).

\subsection{The Blockbuster Dataset}
\label{subsec:methods_blockbuster}

The second dataset, by \citet{ma2021computational}, holds 110 commercially successful films released between 2014 and 2019. Each film is labelled with one or more of six genres: Action, Drama, Comedy, Sci-Fi, Romance and Horror. The dataset is used in two ways. In-domain, the published result is reproduced with the classifier of this thesis, which checks the classifier against an outside benchmark. Across datasets, it is an independent test set of unseen films. This setting tests the claim that emotion generalises.

For copyright reasons, the Blockbuster dataset contains no audio. The authors instead publish features extracted from each musical \emph{cue}, that is, one continuous piece of score music as it is used in the film. Each cue has a 128-dimensional VGGish embedding and 140 hand-crafted MIR descriptors. The dataset contains 4664 cues, with a median of 39 per film and a median length of 19\,s. None of the other representations of Section~\ref{sec:methods_features} can therefore be computed for these films. VGGish is the only representation available on both datasets, so every experiment that crosses them uses it. A cue is also the same kind of object as an Eerola clip: a stretch of score music of similar length. The emotion regressor, trained on Eerola clips, is therefore applied to each cue on its own, and the predicted emotions are averaged per film afterwards. The reverse order would be problematic. If the embeddings were averaged over the film first and emotion predicted only once, the regressor would receive a much smoother vector than any it encountered in training.

\subsection{Genre Labels}
\label{subsec:genre_labels}

Genre is treated as a multi-label problem throughout (Section~\ref{sec:fund_ml}). IMDb lists a film's genres roughly in alphabetical order, not by relevance, so the first genre listed is not a ``primary'' genre. No such notion is used. A clip carries every modelled genre its film is listed with. Clips enter a label space under an \emph{any-present} rule. A clip is kept if at least one of its IMDb genres belongs to the modelled set, and it is then labelled with all genres that do. Clips whose film has no modelled genre (for example, only Fantasy or Mystery) are left out of that label space. They still have emotion ratings, and they are included when Stage~1 is evaluated on its own.

This gives three label spaces, summarised in Table~\ref{tab:label_spaces}. They differ in their genres, their clips and their chance levels, so a score from one space cannot be compared with a score from another. Every result in Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation} is therefore given with its label space and the chance floor of that space.

\paragraph{(a) The eight-genre Eerola space.} Eight genres occur often enough in the Eerola films to be modelled: Action, Adventure, Biography, Comedy, Crime, Documentary, Drama and Horror. Together they give 346 clips from 42 films. The labels are very unbalanced: Drama applies to 239 clips, Documentary to 17.

\paragraph{(b) The five-genre subset.} In earlier work on film audio, the number of genres grows with the size of the dataset. At a scale like this one, \citet{austin2010characterization} model four genres over 98 films. \citet{ma2021computational}, who also work from soundtracks, reduce the IMDb genres to six for their 110 films. The eighteen-genre study of \citet{mangolin2020multimodal} and the nine-genre study of \citet{behrouzi2023multimodal} rest on thousands of films. Compared with these studies, eight genres from 42 soundtracks is a large number, and three of them have little support. Biography and Documentary have fewer than 25 clips each. Adventure is frequent but has no clear emotional signature (every $|d| \le 0.25$; Section~\ref{sec:eval_signatures}), so emotional features are unlikely to recover it. Results are therefore reported on all eight genres and on a chosen subset of five: Action, Crime, Drama, Comedy and Horror. These five are common in the literature and have a measurable emotional signature here. Under the any-present rule the subset contains 329 clips from 40 films. The eight-genre results show the limits set by rare genres and genres without a clear emotional profile. The five-genre results show how the approach performs where it is applicable, and they form the main in-domain setting.

\paragraph{(c) The six-genre space shared with Blockbuster.}
A model can only be transferred between two datasets if it predicts the same classes in both. The two lists of genres differ, however. Crime, Adventure, Biography and Documentary do not exist in the Blockbuster labels, and Sci-Fi and Romance are not among the eight Eerola genres. Both datasets are therefore relabelled into one shared space of six genres: Action, Drama, Comedy, Sci-Fi, Romance and Horror. These six were taken from \citet{ma2021computational}, not chosen here. The rule they used to reduce the IMDb genres to them was recovered from their published list of films and genres, not assumed. It is the identity rule: a film carries the reduced genre $g$ if and only if $g$ occurs in its raw IMDb genre list. This holds for all 110 of their films without exception. It is the same any-present rule already applied to the Eerola labels. Both datasets thus enter the shared space under one rule, without any special handling for either. Sci-Fi and Romance are not among the eight genres modelled on Eerola, because those eight were chosen as the ones frequent enough to learn from Eerola alone. They do, however, occur in the IMDb genre lists of some Eerola films: Romance in seven films (for example \emph{Pride and Prejudice} and \emph{Vertigo}) and Sci-Fi in two (\emph{The Alien Trilogy} and \emph{The Fifth Element}). In the shared space an Eerola clip therefore carries Romance or Sci-Fi whenever its film is listed with it. On Eerola the shared space keeps 319 clips from 40 films. The last two columns of Table~\ref{tab:label_spaces} give how many clips or films carry each genre. With only two Sci-Fi films, that genre rests on very little data on the Eerola side.

\section{Audio Representations}
\label{sec:methods_features}

Both pipelines start from an audio representation (Section~\ref{sec:fund_audio}). Six representations are compared, so that a conclusion about ``direct audio'' does not depend on a single, possibly unsuitable model. They cover different architectures and, above all, different training data. Five are frozen pretrained networks, and one is a hand-crafted baseline with no pretraining. The networks were pretrained on four kinds of task: general sound events (AST, VGGish), matching audio with text (CLAP), music tagging (MusiCNN) and speech (wav2vec~2.0). MusiCNN is included because several of its tags are mood words, so it is expected to be the closest of the five to emotion. wav2vec~2.0 is included because it does not start from a spectrogram. If the other four agree, it shows whether this agreement is only due to their shared mel front-end. Table~\ref{tab:representations} lists the representations used.

The models in Table~\ref{tab:representations} are AST \citep{gong2021ast}, VGGish \citep{hershey2017cnn}, CLAP \citep{wu2023clap}, MusiCNN \citep{pons2019musicnn} and wav2vec~2.0 \citep{baevski2020wav2vec}. MIR stands for the hand-crafted librosa descriptors. ``Input'' is what the model reads, ``Audio used'' is the part of each clip it is computed from, and ``Dim.'' is the length of the vector. MSD stands for the Million Song Dataset.

\paragraph{From a clip to one vector.} Each network turns a clip into a vector in three steps. First, the clip is decoded to mono at the sampling rate the model expects (48\,kHz for CLAP, 16\,kHz for all others). Second, it is passed once through the network. The network is used frozen, as published: nothing is fine-tuned on the thesis data. Most networks do not return one vector for the whole clip but one for each short stretch of time. Third, these vectors are averaged over time into a single vector per clip. For AST this is the output of its last layer. For VGGish it is one vector per 0.96\,s frame. For MusiCNN it is one vector per patch of about 3\,s, taken from its second-to-last layer, which has 200 dimensions. For wav2vec~2.0 it is one vector per short frame. CLAP is the exception: it returns one projected audio embedding per clip, which is used as it is. The VGGish vector is then post-processed (PCA and 8-bit quantisation) in the same way as the features published with the Blockbuster dataset, so that both datasets share one feature space.

\paragraph{The hand-crafted baseline.} The sixth representation uses no network. It is 103 descriptors computed with librosa \citep{mcfee2015librosa}. They are 13 MFCCs and their deltas, chroma, spectral centroid, bandwidth, roll-off, flatness and contrast, zero-crossing rate and root-mean-square (RMS) energy, each summarised by its mean and standard deviation over frames, plus one global tempo estimate. They are not the same as the 140 MIR descriptors of the Blockbuster release, which were computed with other tools.

\paragraph{How much of each clip is used.} AST, MusiCNN and wav2vec~2.0 see the first 10.24\,s of each clip. CLAP's preprocessing takes one 10\,s segment, and VGGish and the MIR descriptors are computed over the whole clip. The average clip is 17\,s long, so the shorter windows discard part of the audio. The effect of this was tested. Each clip was split into consecutive 10.24\,s windows, and the AST embeddings were pooled over all of them (by first, centre, mean or maximum window). The pooling method made no substantial difference to either emotion or genre prediction. The clips are short, uniform pieces of film music, and their first ten seconds appear to describe them well enough for these tasks. The single window was therefore kept for the windowed models. All embeddings are cached per clip, so every later experiment reads the same vectors.

\paragraph{Selecting the layer to pool (wav2vec~2.0).} For the supervised networks, the standard output layer is used. wav2vec~2.0 is different. It is trained with a self-supervised objective on speech, and the last layers of such a model become specialised for that objective. Pooling the last hidden state, the usual default, may therefore be unsuitable for music. All thirteen hidden states (the output of the convolutional encoder and the twelve transformer blocks) were tested on emotion regression under the Stage~1 protocol: random forest, cross-validation grouped by film, mean $R^2$ over the eight emotions. The first three transformer blocks perform best ($R^2$ between 0.294 and 0.297). The last five blocks drop to between 0.154 and 0.170 (0.168 at the final block). The convolutional encoder alone already reaches $0.283$. The choice of layer thus changes the result by almost a factor of two, and the final layer would have understated the quality of the waveform representation considerably. Hidden state 2 is used throughout ($R^2=0.294$ in this sweep, 0.299 in the main run of Table~\ref{tab:waveform_stage1}). It was selected on the emotion task in an earlier run, where it was the best block, and then held fixed. It was never tuned against a genre result. After the film grouping was corrected, the first block is ahead of it by only 0.003. The checkpoint is the self-supervised base model, not a version fine-tuned for speech recognition, whose representations are even more focused on the sounds of speech.

One confound limits what this comparison can show. wav2vec~2.0 differs from the other models both in its input and in its pretraining data, which is speech rather than general audio or music. A weak result for it therefore cannot be attributed to the waveform input alone. MusiCNN covers the opposite case, since its pretraining is the closest to the material.

\section{Stage 1: Predicting Emotion from Audio}
\label{sec:methods_stage1}

The first stage maps an audio embedding to the eight emotion ratings. It is a single random forest regressor with 300 trees that predicts all eight emotions together as one multi-output target. All other settings are library defaults. The same regressor is used for every representation, so the representations are compared on equal terms.

The regressor was chosen by experiment. A random forest, ridge regression and support vector regression (SVR) with a radial basis function (RBF) kernel were compared on all six representations, under the protocol of the Stage-1 evaluation (Appendix~\ref{sec:app_grid}). Section~\ref{sec:fund_ml} explains how ridge regression and SVR work. Ridge regression depends strongly on its regularisation strength $\alpha$. With the library default ($\alpha=1$) it reaches a mean $R^2$ of only 0.385 over the eight emotions on VGGish. With $\alpha$ chosen by cross-validation grouped by film inside the training films, it reaches 0.584, against 0.535 for SVR and 0.553 for the random forest. A first comparison used the default and therefore overstated the advantage of the random forest. On the spectrogram embeddings the three regressors are close, with tuned ridge regression slightly ahead. On MusiCNN and the hand-crafted descriptors the random forest is the most accurate. 

\paragraph{Why VGGish and the random forest.} The main emotion pipeline uses VGGish and the random forest in every experiment, for three reasons. First, VGGish is the only representation available on both datasets (Section~\ref{subsec:methods_blockbuster}). The replication of the signatures and every transfer experiment can only use it. With VGGish, one and the same pipeline serves both datasets, whereas a pipeline on CLAP, for example, could be tested on Eerola only. Second, the pipeline was fixed before the comparison of six representations and three regressors was run (Section~\ref{subsec:eval_grid}). Selecting the best of its 18 entries afterwards would favour whichever entry happened to perform well on these 40 films. The best entry, CLAP with ridge regression, is 0.019 ahead of VGGish with the random forest under seed 42, while the 95\,\% interval of each entry is about 0.13 wide. Third, the random forest needs no tuning of its own and gave the most consistent results across inputs. On VGGish, ridge regression and SVR score 0.002 and 0.003 below it ($p=0.859$ and $p=0.813$), and on the hand-crafted descriptors and wav2vec~2.0 it scores highest. Tuned ridge regression does predict the emotions more accurately on VGGish (0.584 against 0.553), but a more accurate emotion model does not reliably yield a better genre result (Section~\ref{subsec:eval_grid}).

Stage~1 is trained on emotion ratings, which only the Eerola dataset has. When the stage is evaluated on its own, all 360 rated clips of Set~1 are used, including those without a modelled genre. When it feeds the genre classifier, it is fitted only on the clips of the training films of the current fold (in-domain). In the zero-shot experiment it is fitted on the 319 Eerola clips of the shared space, and in the re-implementation of Section~\ref{sec:eval_transfer} on all 360 rated clips. It is then applied to clips or cues it has not seen.

\paragraph{From eight emotions to eleven features.} Three derived features are added to the eight predicted emotions. Together they form the eleven-dimensional input of the genre classifier. Write $v, e, t, a, f, h, s, n$ for valence, energy, tension, anger, fear, happiness, sadness and tenderness. The three features are
\begin{align*}
  x_{\text{v}\times\text{e}} &= v \cdot e, \\
  x_{\text{neg}} &= \tfrac{1}{4}\,(a + f + t + s), \\
  x_{\text{pos}} &= \tfrac{1}{3}\,(h + n + v).
\end{align*}
The product lets a linear classifier respond to combinations such as ``energetic and positive'' that neither scale expresses alone. The two composites summarise the negative and the positive emotions in one value each. All three are fixed functions of the eight emotions and add no information from the audio. For the Blockbuster films, the derived features are computed from the film averages of the emotions predicted per cue.

\paragraph{Why all eight emotions are kept.}
\label{par:methods_eight}
The pipeline keeps all eight emotion scales of the dataset instead of reducing them to valence and arousal. Tension arousal is kept as a feature of its own, because energetic and tense arousal are distinct dimensions (Section~\ref{subsec:fund_why_not_va}). The discrete categories are the more diagnostic description of genre here, but not a necessary one. On the dimensional axes, Action and Horror lie close together (Section~\ref{sec:eval_signatures}). Compared with all other clips, both have lower valence (Cohen's $d = -0.42$ and $-0.63$) and higher tension ($d = +0.46$ and $+0.50$). The discrete categories separate the two genres along one axis. Horror is marked by fear ($d = +0.75$, the strongest link between a genre and an emotion in the dataset). In Action, fear is only moderately raised ($d = +0.35$). Anger does not distinguish them at all ($d = +0.34$ against $+0.38$). In these data, fear is thus the most informative single emotion, and the description ``Horror is fear'' says more about the function of a score than ``Horror is low valence and high tension''.

A classifier does not need the discrete categories, however. Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation} tests this by training the genre classifier on subsets of the eight emotions, and the classifier does not depend on them. Leaving out any single emotion changes the film-level macro-$F_1$ by at most about one hundredth (0.012). The only change that is nominally significant is an increase, when tension is left out. Smaller subsets lose a little, but not significantly: fear alone, or valence and energy alone, keep 96 to 99\,\% of the score of all eight. The eight ratings are strongly correlated with each other (fear rises with tension and falls with valence), so a small subset already contains most of the information relevant to genre. This agrees with the low-dimensional rating space reported by \citet{eerola2011comparison}. All eight are kept for reasons other than prediction. The discrete categories name the function of a score directly, so they make the model easier to interpret. It cannot be known in advance which subset would suffice. Finally, eleven features add almost no cost to computation or modelling. The thesis therefore combines the two frameworks, and it reports that a reduction to fewer emotions would not have lowered accuracy.

\paragraph{Predicted emotions for training the genre stage.} When the genre classifier is trained on predicted emotions, these predictions must be as noisy as the ones it receives at test time. A random forest reproduces its own training clips almost perfectly. Its predictions for them are therefore far more accurate than those for unseen films. A classifier trained on the first and tested on the second would see two different kinds of input. The emotions of the training clips are therefore produced \emph{out-of-fold}. In-domain, they come from an inner five-fold cross-validation, grouped by film, over the training films of each outer fold. For the transfer from Eerola to Blockbuster they come from the same procedure over these 319 clips. An earlier in-domain version of the experiments trained the genre stage on in-sample predictions. Correcting this made no measurable difference ($p=0.37$), but the out-of-fold procedure is the methodologically appropriate one, and it is the one reported.

\section{Stage 2: Predicting Genre from Emotion}
\label{sec:methods_stage2}

The second stage is a binary-relevance classifier (Section~\ref{sec:fund_ml}). It has one L2-regularised logistic regression per genre, and each decides on its own whether the clip carries that genre. The input features are scaled to zero mean and unit variance, with the scaler fitted on the training fold only. Each regression uses balanced class weights. These weight the positive examples of a genre inversely to its frequency, so that a rare genre is not ignored. A clip is assigned a genre when the predicted probability exceeds $0.5$. Sometimes a training fold has no positive example of a rare genre. That genre's classifier then predicts the negative class for the whole fold instead of failing.

The regularisation strength $C$ is chosen from the grid $\{0.003, 0.01, 0.03, 0.1, 0.3, 1\}$. The choice is made by an inner three-fold cross-validation, grouped by film, inside each training fold, and it maximises macro-$F_1$ over the clips of the inner folds. This training procedure was fixed before film-level scoring was adopted for the evaluation, and it was not re-tuned for it. Ties are resolved in favour of the smaller value, which is the simpler model. The test fold never takes part in this choice. The library default $C=1$ was the worst value in the grid for every feature set. It also harmed the large embeddings more than the eleven emotion features, so it would have put the baselines at a disadvantage. Tuning $C$ was therefore needed for a fair comparison. It was not a way of improving the proposed method.

The same classifier, with the same scaler, class weights, threshold and tuning, is used for every input: the eleven emotion features, each direct embedding and the PCA-8 control. Between the arms of an experiment, only the input changes. More flexible classifiers were also tried on the emotion features: a random forest, a multi-layer perceptron, classifier chains that model links between genres, and gradient boosting. None of them outperformed the regularised logistic regression, and gradient boosting overfitted markedly. The linear model is also easier to interpret here, because its weights for each genre on the emotions can be read directly.

\section{Baselines and Controls}
\label{sec:methods_baselines}

Every result is compared with a set of reference arms. Each one addresses a specific question.

\paragraph{Direct audio.} Each of the six representations of Section~\ref{sec:methods_features} is passed to the Stage~2 classifier without any step in between. This is the main comparison of the thesis: how the emotion pipeline compares with classifying genre from the audio representation directly. Six representations are used instead of one, so a positive result is less likely to be due to a weak baseline. In the transfer experiments only VGGish is available on both sides, so it is the only direct arm.

\paragraph{Ground-truth emotion.} The eleven features are computed from the human ratings instead of from predictions. This arm cannot be used in practice, since new films come without ratings. It approximates the ceiling of the emotion pipeline: how well genre could be predicted from emotion if Stage~1 were perfect. The gap between this arm and the arm with predicted emotions estimates the cost of predicting the emotions.

\paragraph{PCA-8 control.} The emotion pipeline compresses the audio into eight values. On a small dataset, any strong compression might help merely by acting as a regulariser. The control tests this (Section~\ref{subsec:fund_bottleneck}). The same VGGish embedding is standardised and projected onto its first eight principal components, with both steps fitted on the training fold only. The result is passed to the same classifier on the same folds. If the emotion pipeline outperformed the direct embedding only because it is small, the PCA-8 control would be expected to outperform it as well. The control is as narrow as the emotion pipeline, but its values have no emotional meaning, and they are defined by the dataset they were fitted on.

\paragraph{Random-8 projection.} On the Blockbuster dataset in-domain, a Gaussian random projection of the VGGish embedding to eight dimensions is added below the PCA-8 control. It is the floor for the control. It shows how much of the information that eight dimensions can carry depends on choosing them well, and thus whether PCA-8 is a serious competitor or only a weak reference.

\begin{table}[tbp]
  \centering
  \small

In Table~\ref{tab:pipelines}, the intermediate step is the only part that differs between the pipelines. The genre classifier is always the same Stage~2 model of Section~\ref{sec:methods_stage2}. \paragraph{Chance floors.} Both chance levels of Section~\ref{sec:fund_evaluation} are computed for every label space, because they differ between the label spaces as well as between the two definitions. On Eerola, Drama applies to more than half of the clips, so the \emph{most-frequent} baseline amounts to predicting ``Drama'' and nothing else. It is the floor for exact match and Hamming loss, where it is difficult to outperform. The \emph{base-rate random guess} is the higher floor for macro-$F_1$, and the more appropriate one. Nothing is trained for it, so it does not go through the cross-validation. Its score is the average over many simulated guesses on the same labels: 0.309 on the five-genre subset. It is one fixed reference value, with no folds and no repeats. On the eight-genre labels, scored per film, the random-guess floor is $0.23$ and the most-frequent baseline $0.10$, so the two differ by more than a tenth of an $F_1$ point. \citet{ma2021computational} report the random guess ($0.32$ on their dataset) and the plurality baseline ($0.19$) separately. Both are reproduced here, so that their results are compared with the right one. Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation} states for each score which of the two floors is meant. A third reference is the \emph{all-positive} classifier, which assigns every genre to every film (Section~\ref{sec:fund_evaluation}). On the five-genre subset it reaches 0.434 over films, above both chance floors. It is not a chance level, but it shows how much of a macro-$F_1$ score can be achieved through recall alone (Section~\ref{sec:eval_genre}).

\section{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:evaluation_protocol}

Section~\ref{sec:fund_evaluation} introduces the ideas behind the protocol: grouped cross-validation, nested tuning, macro-$F_1$, the film bootstrap and the corrected $t$-test. This section describes how they were applied. There are two settings. \emph{In-domain} experiments train and test on different films of the same dataset. They show whether a signal exists in that data. \emph{Transfer} experiments train on one dataset and test on the other. They show whether the signal belongs to film music in general rather than to one collection of films. In all experiments, every step that learns from data (the scaler, the PCA, the emotion regressor and the choice of $C$) is fitted on the training part only.

\subsection{In-domain cross-validation}

On the Eerola dataset, every split is grouped by film, so all clips of one film fall on the same side. This safeguard is necessary. With an ungrouped split, the AST baseline scores 0.44 instead of 0.23 (clip-level macro-$F_1$, eight genres, single untuned five-fold run), because it can recognise the film instead of the genre (Section~\ref{sec:fund_evaluation}). The emotion features lose much less under grouping, as Section~\ref{sec:eval_direct} discusses.

The protocol is five-fold cross-validation grouped by film, repeated ten times. Each repeat assigns the films to folds at random anew. This gives 50 train/test splits that cannot leak. A single five-fold run gives only five scores, which cannot distinguish differences of a few hundredths of macro-$F_1$. All arms are scored on the same 50 folds, so every comparison is paired.

Each repeat is scored once, on its pooled out-of-fold predictions. The five test folds of a repeat together contain every clip exactly once. Their predictions can therefore be joined and macro-$F_1$ computed over all clips. Scoring fold by fold instead is biased. With folds grouped by film, a genre that occurs in few films is missing from many test folds. Comedy, with four films, has no clip in 17 of the 50. Per-fold macro-$F_1$ counts such a genre as zero there, whatever the model predicts. This lowers every model's score by about $0.02$--$0.03$ but does not change the ranking.

The pooled predictions of a repeat are then scored per film. The probabilities predicted for the clips of a film are averaged. The film is assigned every genre whose average probability is at least $0.5$. Macro-$F_1$ is then computed over the films: 40 on the five-genre subset and 42 on eight genres. A film's genre labels are the same for all of its clips. Scoring clips directly would weight each film by its number of clips, which runs from 2 to 18, and would count the clips of one film as separate decisions. Scored per film, each film counts once. The Blockbuster dataset already has one row per film, so every genre result in the thesis is measured on the same unit. The in-domain levels in Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation} are these film-level scores, averaged over the ten repeats. The clip-level macro-$F_1$ of the same predictions is reported next to them. Film-level scoring has one cost. Macro-$F_1$ gives every genre the same weight, and on the five-genre subset Comedy has only 4 films and Horror 5 (on eight genres, Documentary has 2 and Biography 3). A single film therefore changes the $F_1$ of such a genre considerably. The bootstrap intervals below reflect this.

On the Blockbuster dataset in-domain, each row is a whole film. The films are then independent, and plain repeated five-fold cross-validation over films (again $10\times5$, with nested tuning of $C$) is correct. Arms that work on single cues still split by film, and the cues follow their film, since the cues of one film share its label. To reproduce the published result, the protocol of \citet{ma2021computational} is also run exactly as they describe it, with leave-one-film-out cross-validation over the 110 films.

\subsection{Cross-dataset transfer}
\label{subsec:protocol_transfer}

The main transfer experiment is \emph{zero-shot}. Every arm is fitted on the Eerola dataset alone. It is then applied, without any further fitting, to all 110 Blockbuster films. No Blockbuster label is seen at any point; the labels are used only for scoring. Both datasets are expressed in the shared six-genre space (Table~\ref{tab:label_spaces}), so the classifier predicts exactly the classes it was trained on. VGGish is the representation throughout. The two VGGish feature spaces match numerically. Both are the standard post-processed embedding, and their means per dimension correlate at $r=0.97$.

The genre classifier is trained on Eerola clips with out-of-fold predicted emotions (Section~\ref{sec:methods_stage1}), and $C$ is tuned by grouped cross-validation on Eerola. For the Blockbuster films, the emotion arm predicts emotions per cue and averages them per film. The direct and PCA-8 arms use the film's VGGish vector averaged over its cues. The features can be scaled in two ways. In the \emph{strict} regime, the scaling is fitted on the source dataset only and applied unchanged to the target. In the \emph{target-standardised} regime, each dataset is scaled with its own statistics. This is an adaptation that uses no labels. Only the arms based on embeddings differ between the two. The direct baseline scores higher under the strict regime, so this regime is the conservative one, and it is the one quoted.

Two further designs test whether the result depends on the direction of transfer. In the \emph{reverse} direction, the genre classifier is trained on all Blockbuster films and tested on Eerola. The emotion regressor needs ratings, which only Eerola has. It is therefore fitted on the Eerola training fold of a split grouped by film, and the held-out Eerola fold is scored per film, as in-domain. In this way, no test clip enters either fit. In the \emph{pooled} design, both datasets are combined in one table, with Eerola clips and Blockbuster films as rows and the film as the group. It is evaluated by $5\times5$ repeated grouped cross-validation, with the Eerola clips of each test fold scored per film. This design tests whether a model can learn from both datasets together, which neither direction of transfer does.

A transfer experiment is a single train/test split, so its uncertainty cannot come from repeated folds. It is measured by a paired bootstrap over the test films. The 110 films are resampled with replacement 2000 times, and both arms are scored again on each resample, with the fitted models held fixed.

\subsection{Metrics and statistical tests}
\label{subsec:protocol_metrics}

\paragraph{Stage 1.} Emotion regression is scored by the coefficient of determination $R^2$. It is computed for each of the eight emotions on out-of-fold predictions and then averaged. The root mean squared error in rating points is given with it. The predictions come from five-fold cross-validation grouped by film over all 360 rated clips. This mean $R^2$ is the ``feature-extraction accuracy'' by which the six representations are ranked. It measures what the pipeline needs from a representation, namely how much of the variance in the ratings it recovers. This measure is not affected by a second-stage classifier. It is compared with the ceiling of $R^2=0.897$ set by the agreement between the two listener panels (Section~\ref{subsec:methods_eerola}).

\paragraph{Stage 2 and end-to-end.} Genre prediction is scored by macro-averaged $F_1$ (Section~\ref{sec:fund_evaluation}), on one prediction per film as described above. The labels are unbalanced. On the five-genre labels, a classifier that predicts ``Drama'' for every clip reaches an exact-match rate of 0.283 and a Hamming loss of 0.218 over the clips. Both are better than the values of every trained model tested here (at best 0.168 and 0.311), although this classifier has no practical use. Exact match and Hamming loss are therefore computed only for completeness. For the comparison with \citet{ma2021computational}, macro-averaged precision and recall are reported next to macro-$F_1$, as in their paper. They also show whether a model errs by predicting too many genres or too few, which $F_1$ alone does not reveal.

\paragraph{Why macro-$F_1$ and ROC-AUC are read together.} On these data the all-positive classifier lies above both chance floors in macro-$F_1$, so macro-$F_1$ alone cannot show whether a model has learned anything. Both trivial classifiers score exactly 0.5 in ROC-AUC (Section~\ref{sec:fund_evaluation}), but ROC-AUC provides no information about the decisions a model makes, and assigning genres is the task. The thesis therefore keeps macro-$F_1$ as the headline, because it measures the decisions, and reads ROC-AUC beside it as the check. A lead in $F_1$ that also shows in ROC-AUC comes from ranking the films better. A lead in $F_1$ alone may only come from assigning genres more often. \citet{mangolin2020multimodal} prefer the area under the precision--recall curve (AUC-PR) to ROC-AUC, because ROC-AUC can be overly optimistic when the classes are unbalanced. AUC-PR is reported as well, as macro average precision. It gives the same picture: the emotion pipeline ranks first among the audio pipelines, though not significantly ahead of VGGish, and the all-positive classifier only reaches the share of clips with each genre. Appendix~\ref{sec:eval_robustness} shows every metric next to both trivial classifiers.

\paragraph{Confidence intervals and significance.} Intervals for a single arm come from a bootstrap over films. The films are resampled with replacement 2000 times. The film-level predictions are scored again on each draw, and the result is averaged over the ten repeats. Films are the resampling unit because they are the unit that is scored. Draws in which a genre has no positive film are discarded. The interval therefore reflects the limited set of films the dataset happens to contain. An interval over the ten repeat means would only reflect how these films were split into folds, and it would be far too narrow.

Paired comparisons between two arms are reported under two tests, because they answer different questions (Section~\ref{sec:fund_evaluation}). The \emph{paired film bootstrap} applies the resampling above to the difference between the two arms, with the fitted models held fixed. The difference it reports is the mean paired difference over the bootstrap draws. It can therefore differ by up to 0.002 from the difference of the two rounded scores. It is the same test used for the transfer experiments. The \emph{corrected resampled $t$-test} of \citet{nadeau2003inference} (Section~\ref{sec:fund_evaluation}) needs one score per test fold. A test fold, however, holds only about eight films, so most genres have no positive film in it, and a film-level score per fold is of little value. The corrected test is therefore computed on the 50 per-fold clip-level macro-$F_1$ scores of the same predictions. As explained above, the per-fold level is biased low, but the differences between arms are almost unaffected. It is reported as the stricter check beside the film-level bootstrap. Where the two tests disagree, both are reported, and neither is quoted alone. Beside the $p$-values, the thesis gives in how many of the ten repeats one arm scores higher than the other, since this count needs no assumption about distributions. For comparisons that stay non-significant under the corrected test, its limiting value $p_{\lim}$ is reported as well, the lower limit on its $p$-value that more repeats cannot pass (Section~\ref{sec:fund_evaluation}). If $p_{\lim} > 0.05$, no amount of extra resampling could make the comparison significant; only more films could.

\paragraph{Emotional signatures of genres.} To examine which emotions are associated with which genres, the mean rating of each emotion in the clips of a genre is compared with its mean in all other clips. The difference is expressed as Cohen's $d$ (Section~\ref{sec:fund_evaluation}). On Eerola the human ratings are used. On Blockbuster, which has no ratings, the emotions predicted per cue and pooled per film are used. Comparing the two matrices of genres by emotions tests whether the signatures hold on an independent dataset (Section~\ref{sec:eval_signatures}).

\paragraph{Sanity check.} Before any result was interpreted, the extraction pipeline was checked from end to end on an easy side task: predicting the dataset's own twelve balanced discrete emotion categories from the embeddings. These classes are balanced and single-label, so plain accuracy is the appropriate measure there, and chance is exactly $1/12 \approx 0.083$. A score well above it indicates that the cached embeddings belong to the right clips and carry signal.

\subsection{Alternative metrics and the stability of the estimates}
\label{subsec:protocol_altmetrics}

Two questions about the main metric were checked on the five-genre protocol, with the same out-of-fold predictions. Section~\ref{sec:eval_robustness} reports the results.

The first question is whether the ranking of the emotion pipeline, the direct embeddings and the PCA-8 control depends on the choice of macro-$F_1$. Every arm was also scored with the other ways of averaging $F_1$. These are micro (over all clip--genre decisions pooled), weighted by genre frequency, and per clip (``samples''). Every arm was also scored with exact match, Hamming loss and the Jaccard index. The $F_1$ score depends on where the predicted probability is cut, and $0.5$ is only a convention. Macro-$F_1$ was therefore also computed with a separate decision threshold per genre. Each threshold was chosen between $0.05$ and $0.95$ to maximise $F_1$ on out-of-fold probabilities of the training fold (an inner three-fold grouped split), so that the test fold again plays no part. Two metrics avoid the cut completely. Macro average precision (AP) is the area under the precision--recall curve of each genre. Macro ROC-AUC (Section~\ref{sec:fund_evaluation}) is the second. All of these alternatives are computed over clips, as is the clip-level macro-$F_1$ that checks the film-level headline.

The second question is how stable the cross-validated numbers are. Three sources of variation are kept apart. \emph{Split variance} is the standard deviation of the film-level macro-$F_1$ over the ten repeats. At clip level, the spread of the per-fold score over the 50 single folds is given as well. \emph{Sampling variance} is how much a score depends on which films the dataset happens to contain. It is the standard error from the film bootstrap (2000 draws at film level, 1000 in the clip-level comparison of metrics). \emph{Seed variance} is measured by re-running the whole $10\times5$ protocol under five master seeds (42, 1, 2, 3 and 4). Each seed changes both the assignment of films to folds and the random forest. Under seed 42 the re-run reproduces the main in-domain figures exactly, which also checks that the results can be reproduced. The stability analysis is reported at film level, the headline unit, with the clip-level values in the same place.

\section{Implementation and Reproducibility}
\label{sec:methods_implementation}

The implementation is written in Python~3.12 and runs on a central processing unit (CPU). For deep learning, only PyTorch \citep{paszke2019pytorch} is used. AST (checkpoint \texttt{MIT/ast-finetuned-audioset-10-10-0.4593}), CLAP (\texttt{laion/clap-htsat-unfused}) and wav2vec~2.0 (\texttt{facebook/wav2vec2-base}) are loaded through the Hugging Face \texttt{transformers} library \citep{wolf2020transformers}. VGGish is loaded through the \texttt{torchvggish} port. The hand-crafted descriptors are computed with librosa, and all regression, classification and cross-validation uses scikit-learn \citep{pedregosa2011scikit}. MusiCNN is only available for TensorFlow, so it was run in a separate environment (TensorFlow~2.15 on Python~3.11). It is connected to the rest of the project only through the embedding cache, and no other part of the code depends on TensorFlow.

The random seed is fixed at 42 throughout. The embeddings are extracted once, cached per clip and kept under version control. Every result can therefore be reproduced from these cached files without running any neural network over the audio. Each experiment is a self-contained script. Every main experiment writes all its scores per fold and per repeat to a machine-readable results file. Every aggregate in Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation} can thus be derived again without relying on the text. Where a protocol was corrected, the corrected run scores exactly the folds of the earlier run again. It also checks that it reproduces the earlier figures under the old metric, so that any difference can be attributed to the correction alone.
