\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}

% Move 1 (CARS): establishing the research territory
Music is one of the main means by which a film shapes the emotional response of its audience. \citet{gorbman1987unheard} describes film music as a signifier of emotion that works through a system of learned codes. Minor keys and dissonant harmony, for example, have come to signal threat or suspense, while other choices of harmony, rhythm and instrumentation are associated with romance, comedy or heroism. \citet{juslin2013everyday} place such codes within a broader account of how music conveys emotion, in which learned associations allow music to evoke emotional meanings that are shared within a culture. \citet{austin2010characterization} make a related observation from the side of film genre. Films are designed to evoke emotion, and the scores of high-intensity genres such as Action and Horror differ measurably from those of genres with a more restrained emotional expression, such as Drama and Romance. Section~\ref{sec:fund_motivation_theory} discusses these accounts in more detail.

Two lines of computational research build on these observations. Music emotion recognition (MER) predicts the emotion that listeners perceive in a piece of music from its audio \citep{yang2012machine,kim2010music,kang2025there}. Film genre classification predicts the genres of a film from its audio, its images or its text \citep{austin2010characterization,ma2021computational,mangolin2020multimodal,behrouzi2023multimodal}. The second task has practical value. Streaming providers have a growing interest in deriving the genres of films automatically, and genre labels support the retrieval and recommendation of films \citep{mangolin2020multimodal,behrouzi2023multimodal}.

% Move 2 (CARS): establishing a niche
So far, the two lines of research have remained separate. The film genre studies reviewed in Chapter~\ref{ch:relatedwork} predict genre directly from an audio representation: from low-level features of the score \citep{austin2010characterization}, from learned embeddings of the music cues of a film \citep{ma2021computational}, or from trailer audio combined with images and text \citep{mangolin2020multimodal,behrouzi2023multimodal}. None of them includes an explicit emotional step. Such models may predict genre accurately, but they offer no direct explanation of why a soundtrack is assigned a particular genre. Their input is a vector of several hundred values without a fixed meaning, which does not correspond to the emotional mechanism described in film-music research. At the same time, music research reports that emotion and genre are closely related \citep{hu2007exploring,laurier2009mood,eerola2011genrespecific}. To my knowledge, it has not yet been tested whether emotion can serve as an intermediate step between film audio and film genre.

Such a step could offer two advantages. First, a prediction that rests on emotions can, in principle, be explained and checked. A model that predicts Horror because the music is rated as fearful and tense can be compared with established practice in film scoring \citep{gorbman1987unheard}. Second, emotion ratings are given on fixed scales that are defined independently of any particular dataset, whereas the dimensions of a learned audio embedding reflect the data on which the embedding was computed. A model built on emotions may therefore transfer better to a new collection of films. Neither advantage is certain. Reducing a clip to a small number of emotions discards information, and the emotions must first be predicted from the audio, which remains difficult, in particular for valence \citep{kim2010music,kang2025there}. Whether an explicit emotional step improves genre prediction, leaves it unchanged or impairs it is therefore an empirical question.

% Move 3 (CARS): occupying the niche (continued in Sections 1.2 and 1.3)
I address it with the following question: \emph{how well can
the emotional features of a film soundtrack predict the genre of the film, and how does
modelling emotion as an interpretable intermediate step compare with classifying genre
directly from audio?}

\section{Aim of this thesis}
\label{sec:aim}

The aim is to examine how well soundtrack emotion serves as an interpretable step between
film audio and film genre. Four research questions structure the work, and Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} answers each of
them (Section~\ref{sec:disc_rq}).

The first research question (\textbf{RQ1}) asks \emph{how well the emotions expressed by a
soundtrack predict its film's genre}. It concerns the strength of the signal and is answered
twice: first with emotions rated by human listeners, then with emotions predicted from audio.
The second (\textbf{RQ2}) asks \emph{which emotions characterise each genre, and how far
these emotional signatures hold on a second, independent dataset}. It concerns the form of
the signal and examines whether the emotional step can be interpreted in the way the
motivation above suggests. The third (\textbf{RQ3}) asks \emph{how predicting genre from
emotions compares with predicting it directly from the same audio representation}. It is the
central comparison of the thesis and is made within one dataset. The fourth (\textbf{RQ4})
asks \emph{how well the two pipelines generalise when a model trained on one dataset is
applied to another} without access to any of its labels. RQ2 and RQ4 both use the second
dataset, but for different purposes. RQ2 asks whether the link between emotions and genres
stays the same there, and RQ4 whether a genre classifier still works there.

Two datasets are used. The first, by \citet{eerola2011comparison}, contains 360
film-music clips from 45 films. Listeners rated each clip on eight emotion scales, and
the genres of the films come from the Internet Movie Database (IMDb). The second, by
\citet{ma2021computational}, contains 110 recent films. Each film is described by features of the music cues it uses, published by the authors: VGGish embeddings, a learned audio representation, and hand-crafted music information retrieval (MIR) descriptors. The dataset contains no emotion ratings and no audio. The
method has two steps. A random forest predicts the eight emotions from a pre-trained
audio representation (VGGish in the main experiments). A logistic regression for each
genre then predicts the genres from these emotions. This emotion pipeline is compared with
classifying genre from the same audio representation directly. It is also compared with
a control that compresses the representation into as many values as there are emotions,
without giving those values any emotional meaning. Only the music is used, with no
images, dialogue or plot, and the pre-trained audio models are used as they are, without
fine-tuning.

\section{Structure of this thesis}
\label{sec:structure}

Chapter~\ref{ch:fundamentals} is written for readers new to the field. It explains the
models of emotion, the audio representations, the two learning methods and the evaluation
methods. Chapter~\ref{ch:relatedwork} reviews the surveys of music emotion
recognition, the evidence that emotion and genre are linked in music, and the four
film-genre studies this work is compared with. Chapter~\ref{ch:methods} describes the two
datasets, the genre labels, the two-stage pipeline, the baselines and controls, and the
evaluation protocol. Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation} reports the experiments in the order
of the pipeline. Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} answers the research questions, interprets the
results and discusses the limits of the work. Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusions} summarises the
answers and outlines directions for future work. Appendix~\ref{app:supplementary} reports further checks of the main
results.
