\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}

A few seconds of a soundtrack are often enough to guess what kind of film it comes from.
Harsh string chords over a slow, low pulse point to horror. Loud brass over fast, heavy
drums points to action. A solo piano over soft strings sounds like drama or romance.
Listeners make this guess without seeing a single frame, and they are often sure of it.
This thesis starts from the idea that the guess is not really about genre at all. The
listener first hears an emotion, such as threat, excitement, tenderness or sadness, and
then works out the genre from it.

This works because film composers write music to set the emotional tone of a scene and
of a whole film. \citet{gorbman1987unheard} describes film music as a system of learned
codes. Minor keys and dissonant harmony have come to signal threat or suspense. Other
choices of harmony, rhythm and instruments are tied to romance, comedy or heroism.
\citet{juslin2013everyday} fits such codes into a wider account of how music carries
emotion: learned associations let music call up emotional meanings that a culture
shares. In film scoring, these meanings are closely bound to genre.
Section~\ref{sec:fund_motivation_theory} looks at both accounts more closely. For this
thesis the point is simple. Composers put the link between soundtrack and genre into the
emotion of the music, so that is where a model should look for it.

Predicting genre from film audio is an established task, and a useful one. Genre labels
organise film archives and streaming libraries, feed recommendation systems and help
people find music that suits a scene. Existing work treats it as pure pattern
recognition, though. \citet{austin2010characterization} classify film genre from
low-level features of the score, and \citet{ma2021computational} from learned audio
embeddings of the music cues in a film. \citet{mangolin2020multimodal} and
\citet{behrouzi2023multimodal} combine trailer audio with images and text.
Chapter~\ref{ch:relatedwork} reviews these studies. All of them predict genre
\emph{directly} from an audio representation. Such a model may be accurate, but it cannot
say why a soundtrack sounds like Horror. It works with a vector of several hundred numbers
that have no fixed meaning. Nothing in it matches the emotional mechanism that composers
and film-music scholars describe.

So there is a gap between what we know about film music and how it is modelled. If
emotion is what links a soundtrack to its genre, modelling the emotion explicitly should
cost little accuracy. The result should also be far easier to understand. A model that
predicts Horror because the music is fearful and tense can be checked against what
composers actually do, and a person can question and correct it. That is impossible when
the reason is that dimension 87 of an embedding is large. There is a second, less obvious
reason why emotions might help. An emotion rating means the same thing in every dataset:
a fearful cue is fearful whichever film collection it comes from. The dimensions of a
learned audio embedding, on the other hand, carry the statistics of the data they were
computed on. A model built on emotions might therefore cope better with a new dataset
than a model built on raw embeddings.

Neither hope is guaranteed. Reducing a clip to a handful of emotions throws a lot away,
and that only pays off if what is kept matters more than what is lost. The emotions also
have to be predicted from the audio first, and emotion prediction from audio is far from
perfect. Whether an explicit emotional step helps, costs nothing or simply loses
information is therefore a question for experiment. This thesis asks: \emph{how well can
the emotional features of a film soundtrack predict the genre of the film, and how does
modelling emotion as an interpretable intermediate step compare with classifying genre
directly from audio?}

\section{Aim of this thesis}
\label{sec:aim}

The aim is to find out how well soundtrack emotion works as an interpretable step between
film audio and film genre. Four research questions (RQ1 to RQ4) structure the work.
Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} answers each of them (Section~\ref{sec:disc_rq}).

\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1] How well do the emotions expressed by a soundtrack predict its film's genre?
  \item[RQ2] Which emotions characterise each genre, and how far do these emotional
    signatures hold on a second, independent dataset?
  \item[RQ3] How does a pipeline that predicts genre from emotions compare with
    classifying genre directly from the audio representation the emotions are computed
    from?
  \item[RQ4] How well do the two pipelines generalise when a model trained on one dataset
    is applied to another?
\end{description}

RQ1 asks how strong the signal is. It is answered first with emotions rated by human
listeners and then with emotions predicted from audio. RQ2 is about the shape of the
signal: which emotions set each genre apart, and whether the same emotional signatures
turn up in a second collection of films. This checks whether the emotional step can be
read the way the motivation above suggests. RQ3 is the central comparison of the thesis,
made within one dataset. RQ4 asks how the two pipelines do when a model trained on one
collection of films is applied to another without seeing any of its labels. RQ2 and RQ4
both use the second dataset, but they ask different things. RQ2 asks whether the link
between emotions and genres stays the same there. RQ4 asks whether a genre classifier
still works there.

Two datasets are used. The first, by \citet{eerola2011comparison}, contains 360
film-music clips from 45 films. Listeners rated each clip on eight emotion scales, and
the genres of the films come from the Internet Movie Database (IMDb). The second, by
\citet{ma2021computational}, contains 110 recent films. Each film is described by audio
features of the music cues it uses, but there are no emotion ratings and no audio. The
method has two steps. A random forest predicts the eight emotions from a pre-trained
audio representation (VGGish in the main experiments). A logistic regression for each
genre then predicts the genres from these emotions. This emotion pipeline is compared with
classifying genre from the same audio representation directly. It is also compared with
a control that squeezes the representation into as many numbers as there are emotions,
without giving those numbers any emotional meaning. Only the music is used, with no
images, dialogue or plot, and the pre-trained audio models are used as they are, without
fine-tuning.

\section{Structure of this thesis}
\label{sec:structure}

Chapter~\ref{ch:fundamentals} is written for readers new to the field. It explains the
models of emotion, the audio representations, the two learning methods and the ways the
results are measured. Chapter~\ref{ch:relatedwork} reviews the surveys of music emotion
recognition, the evidence that emotion and genre are linked in music, and the four
film-genre studies this work is compared with. Chapter~\ref{ch:methods} describes the two
datasets, the genre labels, the two-stage pipeline, the baselines and controls, and how
the evaluation is done. Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation} reports the experiments in the order
of the pipeline. Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} answers the research questions, interprets the
results and discusses the limits of the work. Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusions} sums up the
answers and names the next steps. Appendix~\ref{app:supplementary} reports the checks of the main results,
none of which changes them.
