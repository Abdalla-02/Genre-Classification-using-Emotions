\chapter{Discussion}
\label{ch:discussion}

Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation} reported the results. This chapter interprets them.
Section~\ref{sec:disc_rq} answers the four research questions.
Section~\ref{sec:disc_main} discusses possible reasons why the emotion step helps in some settings
and not in others, and Section~\ref{sec:disc_bottleneck} considers what the emotions contribute beyond
reducing the size of the input. Section~\ref{sec:disc_limitations} states the limitations, and
Section~\ref{sec:disc_relatedwork} relates the results to the literature.

\section{Answers to the Research Questions}
\label{sec:disc_rq}

\paragraph{RQ1: How well do the emotions expressed by a soundtrack predict its film's genre?}
The emotions predict genre well above chance, but far from perfectly. With the human ratings, the logistic regression
reaches a film-level macro-$F_1$ of 0.491 on five genres, and the full emotion pipeline
(VGGish, random forest, logistic regression) 0.460, against 0.309 for a random guess
(Section~\ref{sec:eval_genre}). The distance to the chance floors overstates the predictive ability,
however. A classifier that assigns every genre to every film already reaches 0.434. A more
informative measure is the ranking: the emotion pipeline orders about two of every three pairs
of films correctly (ROC-AUC 0.655), whereas that classifier cannot order any. Emotions
predicted from audio appear to retain most of the genre information in the ratings they were
trained on. Once trained, the pipeline needs no emotion labels, although a better
emotion model might still yield a small improvement. Stage~1 itself reaches 62\,\% of the $R^2$ that the
agreement between two listener panels allows. This suggests that noise in the ratings is not
the main limitation of the regressor, and that the limit lies more in the audio
representations and the amount of training data.

\paragraph{RQ2: Which emotions characterise each genre, and how far do these signatures
hold?} Most genres have an emotional signature that is consistent with the conventions of film
scoring (Section~\ref{sec:eval_signatures}). Horror is characterised mainly by fear, Comedy by the
absence of fear together with positive valence, Drama by raised valence, and Action by high
tension without happiness. Fear is the most discriminating emotion overall, followed by
valence. Adventure has no signature, possibly because it occurs together with several other genres
and shares their emotional range. The signatures largely hold on the Blockbuster films
($r=0.836$), which suggests that the structure of the link between emotion and genre extends
beyond the dataset in which it was first observed. One caveat remains. The Blockbuster emotions are predicted by a model
trained on the Eerola panel, so the replication cannot show independence from that panel.

\paragraph{RQ3: How does the emotion pipeline compare with classifying genre directly from
audio within one dataset?} The answer has two parts. At the default threshold the emotion
pipeline is consistently ahead of direct audio, in nearly every repeat and under every seed
(Section~\ref{sec:eval_direct}). The size of its lead over the strongest direct
representations, however, cannot be estimated reliably on 40 films. The advantage does not appear to depend on
VGGish or on the random forest. It appears with each of six representations and three
regressors, which points to the emotion step rather than to one particular model (Section~\ref{subsec:eval_grid}). Even
wav2vec~2.0, whose emotions are the least accurate, gives a genre result on par with VGGish
once its output passes through the emotions. This suggests that the genre classifier does not
depend mainly on the most accurate estimate per clip from Stage~1.

\paragraph{RQ4: How well do the pipelines generalise to another dataset?} Much of the genre
information transfers: trained on Eerola alone, the emotion pipeline reaches 0.508 on the 110
Blockbuster films, about 82\,\% of what in-domain training reaches
(Section~\ref{sec:zero_shot}). At the default threshold it transfers better than direct
VGGish ($+0.106$, $p=0.013$). Most of that lead in $F_1$, however, depends on the position
of the threshold. With thresholds tuned on the source it shrinks to $+0.041$ ($p=0.159$). What
remains is the ranking of films, where the emotion pipeline stays ahead, although not
significantly. The reverse
direction agrees in sign but is not significant, and with both datasets in training the
pipelines are on par.

\section{Genre Prediction with Little Data and across Datasets}
\label{sec:disc_main}

The emotion pipeline is not better everywhere. Table~\ref{tab:disc_settings} shows a consistent
pattern. All three pipelines in it start from VGGish and end in the same genre classifier, a
logistic regression per genre (Section~\ref{sec:methods_stage2}). The emotion pipeline is
well ahead of direct audio where this classifier was trained on the 40 Eerola films. It is
on par with direct audio where the classifier was trained on the 110 Blockbuster films, or
on both datasets together. The pooled design shows both effects at once. On the Eerola films
of its test folds the emotion pipeline leads, and on the Blockbuster films direct audio
does. The advantage of the emotion pipeline therefore appears to be an advantage when training
data are scarce and when the dataset changes. This is a narrower claim than ``emotion predicts
genre better than audio'', and it is the claim that these results support most directly.

Three possible explanations are compatible with this pattern, and the experiments do not
separate them. First, with about 40 training films, a linear
classifier over 128 to 768 dimensions can fit many directions that happen to separate the
genres of the training films. The emotion stage may add prior knowledge from a second,
independent source of supervision: the listener ratings. This is consistent with the pattern above, where
direct audio reaches the same level exactly where 110 training films are available. It is also consistent with the
comparison over representations. There the emotion step gains most where the direct
representation is weakest (AST and wav2vec~2.0, by 0.13 to 0.21) and least over MusiCNN
(0.03 to 0.05). Second, a rich embedding also encodes the sound of single films: their
instruments, recording and mixing. This information is likely of little use when the evaluation is grouped by film.
An emotion rating, in contrast, describes what a clip expresses, not how it was produced.
Third, an embedding's dimensions carry the statistics of the recordings they were computed
on. Emotion values are given on a rating scale that is intended to mean the same in any dataset. With
both datasets in the training data, the direct classifier sees both sets of statistics, and
its disadvantage disappears.

\paragraph{The decision threshold.} Much of the advantage depends on the threshold of 0.5.
The emotion pipeline leads mainly through recall on the rare genres
(Section~\ref{sec:eval_pergenre}). It identifies them, at the cost of more false positives.
With a threshold per genre tuned on the training folds the pipelines converge
in-domain, and the zero-shot lead shrinks and is no longer significant. The probabilities of
the emotion pipeline also lie close to one half, so a small shift of the threshold changes
its decisions considerably (Appendix~\ref{sec:eval_robustness}). A model that performs well at the default
threshold may still have a practical advantage. Tuning per genre requires labelled data, and it
becomes unreliable for genres with only a few films. On a new dataset, too, the
emotion pipeline changes little when the threshold is tuned, while direct audio requires the
tuning to reach a similar level. Nevertheless, this advantage is smaller and more specific than a better
ranking of films. In the reverse direction the emotion pipeline is ahead in both precision
(0.407 against 0.362) and recall (0.631 against 0.490). A shift of the threshold alone
would not produce this, but the difference is not significant over 40 test films.

\section{What the Emotions Add}
\label{sec:disc_bottleneck}

\paragraph{Meaning or compression?} One possible objection is that eleven features simply overfit
less than 128 on a dataset of about 330 clips. The evidence is mixed. In-domain at film
level, the PCA-8 control gains over the embedding as well, so compression may account for part
of the gain. The emotion pipeline nevertheless stays ahead of the control under every seed. Under
transfer the evidence is clearer in the original zero-shot run. There, compressing the
embedding to eight principal components gains $+0.013$ ($p=0.737$), and compressing it to
eight emotions gains $+0.106$. In a second implementation of the same direction, the lead
over the control is not significant. A further indication that the emotions contribute more
than a small number of dimensions comes from the replication of the signatures. The genre profiles derived from
human ratings on Eerola reappear on Blockbuster from predicted emotions, and Horror is
characterised by fear in both. A principal component has no predefined meaning that could replicate in this
way.

\subsection{A low-dimensional genre signal}
\label{subsec:disc_ablation}

The ablation (Appendix~\ref{sec:app_ablation}) suggests that no single emotion is necessary.
One to three emotions keep most of the genre information. The genre signal of the ratings
appears to lie mainly along one axis, from threat to warmth, made of fear, tension and valence.
This may be one reason why richer representations did not extract more genre information from the music with the
data available. Through the emotions, all six representations end within 0.07 of one
another. All eight emotions are kept because the discrete categories make the model easier
to interpret. ``Horror is fear'' describes a score better than ``Horror is low valence and high
tension'', although a classifier can use either. The same axis may also explain why the imperfect
emotion predictions perform almost as well as the ratings. Fear, tension and valence are among
the emotions predicted best, while the hardest ones, happiness and sadness, add little for
genre. The ratings are ahead at film level, but not significantly (0.032, $p=0.277$). A
better emotion model might therefore help slightly, but by much less than its gap in $R^2$
suggests. A more accurate regressor did not reliably improve the genre result. Ridge regression predicts the
emotions better than the random forest on four of the six representations. Over five
seeds, however, its genre result is consistently higher only on CLAP, and it is lower on
VGGish and wav2vec~2.0 (Section~\ref{subsec:eval_grid}).

\subsection{Interpretability of the bottleneck}

Apart from accuracy, the emotion pipeline was designed to be interpretable, and the results
suggest that it is, at least for the genres it predicts well. Each of these genres has an
interpretable emotional signature. The results per genre
show where this helps and where it does not. The emotion pipeline gains most on Comedy,
whose signature is the absence of fear, and on Action. Drama, the genre of most films, is
predicted well by every pipeline and separates them least. Adventure, the one genre without
a signature, is predicted poorly relative to its frequency.

Horror illustrates how an error can be traced. Its signature is the strongest, yet the emotion
pipeline identifies only half of the Horror films and predicts the genre for too many others.
The weakness appears to come mainly from Stage~1 rather than from the genre classifier, which
reaches 0.41 on Horror from the ratings. The random forest shifts the extreme fear of the Horror clips towards the
average. They are rated at 4.35 on average but predicted at 3.73, close to the predicted
fear of the Action clips (3.46). The distance between the two genres thus shrinks from 0.93
to 0.27. Predicted energy also rises in Horror clips. In the space of predicted emotions,
Horror therefore resembles a tense action or crime film (Appendix~\ref{sec:app_horror}).
For both the successes and the errors of the pipeline, a candidate explanation can be
given and checked against how film music is written.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:disc_limitations}

\paragraph{Dataset size.} The Eerola dataset contains 360 rated clips from 45 films. Of
these, 42 films have a modelled genre (40 in the five-genre subset). Comedy and Horror have
only 4 and 5 films. Their film-level $F_1$ therefore changes considerably when a single film
changes, and the bootstrap error is of the same order as the effects being measured. The
results per genre are therefore a description, not a test. For the borderline clip-level
comparisons, the Nadeau--Bengio test could not become significant with any number of
repeats. Only more films could resolve them. The learning curves were still rising at the
full dataset size.

\paragraph{Unit of evaluation.} Scoring per film was adopted after the clip-level analysis
had been done. At that point the analysis of alternative metrics suggested that it fits the
structure of the labels. A metric chosen after the results are known can favour the method
it is chosen for. The clip-level score of the same predictions is therefore reported next
to it. The direction of every main conclusion is the same under both. Which comparisons
reach significance differs. Over clips the film bootstrap finds the in-domain lead
significant against VGGish, CLAP, MusiCNN and the PCA-8 control; over films it does not.
For AST it is the other way round. Film-level scoring therefore does not appear to favour the
emotion pipeline. For most comparisons it is the stricter of the two. The reverse
direction of transfer is not significant under either, once films are resampled.

\paragraph{Tests and operating point.} At film level only the film bootstrap can be
computed. Over clips, the stricter Nadeau--Bengio test finds the in-domain advantage
borderline. Both are reported because they measure different sources of uncertainty. The
main results are obtained at the default decision threshold, where the advantage is
clearest. At that threshold, a classifier that assigns every genre to every film is also
difficult to outperform. It reaches 0.434 on the five-genre subset, only 0.022 below the emotion
pipeline. On 40 films, macro-$F_1$ depends heavily on how often a pipeline assigns a genre, so the
threshold-free results should be read beside it.

\paragraph{Choice of the five-genre subset.} The five headline genres were chosen partly
because they carry an emotional signature in the same data. This can favour the emotion
pipeline. The eight-genre results reduce this risk but do not remove it. There the
advantage over VGGish and AST is of similar size and significant at film level.

\paragraph{Labels and listeners.} Genre labels come from IMDb and describe the film, not
the single clip. Some clips therefore carry labels that do not describe their music. IMDb
lists genres alphabetically, so there is no primary genre that could weight the labels. The
emotion model learns perceived emotion from the ratings of one group of listeners.
Listeners from a different musical culture might rate the same clips differently.

\paragraph{The second dataset.} The Blockbuster dataset shares neither audio nor emotion
ratings. The transfer therefore rests on a single representation (VGGish), and the
Blockbuster emotions are checked only indirectly. No film occurs in both datasets, but some
composers do, so stylistic overlap across the split cannot be ruled out. The zero-shot
comparison is a single split. Its point estimates change by a few hundredths with legitimate
choices in the implementation, so it rests on its confidence intervals.

\section{Relation to Prior Work}
\label{sec:disc_relatedwork}

The comparison with \citet{ma2021computational} is direct, since it uses their data and protocol. Their protocol,
reproduced on their published features, reaches 0.620 in-domain, inside their reported
range. Under repeated cross-validation the learned embedding does not outperform their full set
of hand-crafted features. This is consistent with their own cautious wording. It is also consistent with
the result on Eerola, where direct representations of very different design score close
to one another. In Stage~1, arousal-like scales are predicted best and happiness and
sadness worst. This mirrors the ``valence problem'' of music emotion recognition: audio
conveys arousal more reliably than whether an emotion is positive or negative
\citep{kim2010music,kang2025there}. \citet{eerola2011genrespecific} reported that an emotion
model trained on one musical genre works much worse on another. Here the same applies to
genre models transferred from one film dataset to another. What this thesis adds is the observation that a model built on
emotions lost less in this setting. This suggests that the dependence on the repertoire lies more in the
mapping from audio to its representation than in the link between emotion and genre.
\citet{austin2010characterization} reported that intense genres were easier to separate by their
scores than story-driven genres, and the signatures observed here offer a possible
explanation. Genres with a conventional emotional identity could be recovered from the music
here, while genres defined mainly by story or setting could not. \citet{mangolin2020multimodal} reported audio as the weakest of
five kinds of input for genre, and the modest absolute scores reported here are consistent
with that. The
contribution of this thesis is therefore not a higher score. It is a model whose
predictions can be related to emotions and which lost less when the data changed. This offers
one response to the concern of \citet{kang2025there} that accurate emotion-recognition systems reveal little
about how they work: used as a step in between, the emotion model provides a partial
explanation of the genre prediction.
Finally, the metric analysis explains why Hamming loss, the headline metric of
\citet{behrouzi2023multimodal}, is not used here. On these data, a classifier that always
predicts the most frequent genres achieves the best value on it.
