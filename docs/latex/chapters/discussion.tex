\chapter{Discussion}
\label{ch:discussion}

Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation} reported the results. This chapter interprets them.
Section~\ref{sec:disc_main} puts the main comparison of all settings side by side.
Section~\ref{sec:disc_bottleneck} discusses why the emotion bottleneck helps where it does.
Section~\ref{sec:disc_limitations} states the limitations, and
Section~\ref{sec:disc_relatedwork} relates the findings to the literature.

\section{Interpreting the Main Result}
\label{sec:disc_main}

Table~\ref{tab:disc_settings} lists the emotion pipeline, the PCA-8 control and direct
audio for every setting in which all three were run. All three start from VGGish and end
in the same logistic-regression classifier. In between, the emotion pipeline predicts the
eight emotions with a random forest, and the control compresses the embedding to eight
principal components. Direct audio has no step in between. Every score is film-level
macro-$F_1$, and each row is compared only with the chance floor of its own label space.

In Table~\ref{tab:disc_settings}, $\Delta$ is the emotion pipeline minus direct audio. The
$p$-value is the paired film bootstrap unless marked $^{\dagger}$. That mark means the
Nadeau--Bengio corrected $t$-test over repeated folds; for the Eerola in-domain row it is
computed on the clip-level scores of the same predictions. The zero-shot row is the strict
(source-scaler) regime.

The table shows one pattern. The emotion pipeline is well ahead of direct audio where the
genre classifier was trained on the 40 Eerola films. On the 110 Blockbuster films the lead
is significant, and on unseen Eerola films it just misses significance ($p=0.052$). Where
the classifier was trained on the Blockbuster films, in-domain there or with both datasets
in training, the emotion pipeline is level with direct audio. When a classifier trained on
Blockbuster is tested on Eerola, it is ahead but not significantly. The pooled design shows
both sides at once. On the Eerola films of its test folds the emotion pipeline leads (0.528
against 0.410), and on the Blockbuster films direct audio does (0.579 against 0.568). The
advantage of the emotion pipeline is therefore an advantage when training data are scarce
and when the dataset changes. This is a narrower claim than ``emotion predicts genre better
than audio'', and it is the one the results support.

\paragraph{In-domain.} On the five-genre subset the emotion pipeline is ahead of every
direct representation and of the PCA-8 control, in nearly every repeat and under every
seed. It is also ahead with each of the six representations as input and with each of
three emotion regressors (Section~\ref{subsec:eval_grid}). The advantage is thus not tied
to VGGish or to the random forest. Two caveats apply. First, the lead is significant only
against the weaker direct representations. Against VGGish it just misses significance at
film level ($p=0.052$). Against the strongest ones and the PCA-8 control it is not
significant, and the PCA-8 control is itself ahead of VGGish under every seed. Part of the
in-domain gain therefore comes from compressing the embedding, not from what the eight
numbers mean. Second, part of it comes from the operating point. The emotion pipeline wins
mainly by recall on the rare genres (Section~\ref{sec:eval_pergenre}). With a threshold
per genre tuned on the training folds, the pipelines come together
(Appendix~\ref{sec:eval_robustness}). A model that works at the default threshold does
have a practical advantage. Tuning per genre needs labelled data, and it becomes unreliable
for genres with only a handful of films. Still, this advantage is smaller and more specific
than a better ranking of films.

\paragraph{Across datasets.} Trained on Eerola and applied without further fitting to the
Blockbuster films, the emotion pipeline beats direct audio by $+0.106$ ($p=0.013$) and the
PCA-8 control by $+0.093$ ($p=0.027$). Its lead is again partly driven by recall. In the
reverse direction it is ahead in both precision (0.407 against 0.362) and recall (0.631
against 0.490). A shift of the decision threshold alone cannot do that. The difference in
$F_1$ is not significant over 40 test films, though. Whether the zero-shot advantage
survives a threshold tuned on the source dataset was not tested
(Section~\ref{sec:futurework}).

\section{Why the Bottleneck Helps Where It Does}
\label{sec:disc_bottleneck}

\paragraph{Meaning or compression?} A sceptic might say that eleven numbers simply overfit
less than 128 on a dataset of about 330 clips. The evidence is mixed. In-domain at film
level, the PCA-8 control gains over the embedding as well, so compression explains part of
the gain. The emotion pipeline still stays ahead of the control under every seed. Under
transfer the picture is clearer in the original zero-shot run. There, compressing the
embedding to eight principal components gains $+0.013$ ($p=0.737$), and compressing it to
eight emotions gains $+0.106$. In a second implementation of the same direction, the lead
over the control is not significant. The strongest evidence that the emotions mean more
than their number is the replication of the signatures. The genre profiles derived from
human ratings on Eerola reappear on Blockbuster from predicted emotions ($r=0.836$), and
Horror is defined by fear in both. A principal component has no meaning that could
replicate in this way.

\paragraph{Scarce data, film identity and domain shift.} The direct embeddings contain
strictly more information than the eight emotions predicted from them. Yet they do worse
on the small Eerola dataset and across datasets. Three explanations support each other.
First, with about 40 training films, a linear classifier over 128 to 768 dimensions can fit
many directions that happen to separate the genres of the training films. The emotion stage
adds prior knowledge from a second, independent source of supervision: the listener
ratings. This fits the pattern of Table~\ref{tab:disc_settings}, where direct audio catches
up exactly where 110 training films are available. It also fits the comparison over
representations. There the emotion step gains most where the direct representation is
weakest (AST and wav2vec~2.0, by 0.13 to 0.21) and least over MusiCNN (0.03 to 0.05).
Second, a rich embedding also encodes the sound of single films: their instruments,
recording and mixing. This is useless when the evaluation is grouped by film. An emotion
rating, in contrast, describes what a clip expresses, not how it was produced. Third, an
embedding's dimensions carry the statistics of the recordings they were computed on.
Emotion values are given on a rating scale that means the same in any dataset. With both
datasets in the training data, the direct classifier sees both sets of statistics, and
its handicap is gone.

\subsection{A low-dimensional genre signal}
\label{subsec:disc_ablation}

The ablation (Appendix~\ref{sec:app_ablation}) shows that no single emotion is necessary.
One to three emotions keep most of the genre information. The genre signal of the ratings
lies along essentially one axis, from threat to warmth, made of fear, tension and valence.
This is one reason why richer representations cannot get more out of the music with the
data available. Through the emotions, all six representations end within 0.07 of one
another. All eight emotions are kept because the discrete categories make the model easy
to read. ``Horror is fear'' describes a score better than ``Horror is low valence and high
tension'', although a classifier can use either. The same axis explains why the imperfect
emotion predictions work almost as well as the ratings. Fear, tension and valence are among
the emotions predicted best, while the hardest ones, happiness and sadness, add little for
genre. The ratings are ahead at film level, but not significantly (0.032, $p=0.277$). A
better emotion model might therefore help a little, but by much less than its gap in $R^2$
suggests. A more accurate regressor did not reliably help. Ridge regression predicts the
emotions better than the random forest on four of the six representations. Over five
seeds, however, its genre result is consistently higher only on CLAP, and it is lower on
VGGish and wav2vec~2.0 (Section~\ref{subsec:eval_grid}).

\subsection{What the bottleneck buys in readability}

Apart from accuracy, the emotion pipeline gives what it was designed for. Each genre the
model predicts well has an emotional signature that can be read. The results per genre
show where this helps and where it does not. The emotion pipeline gains most on Comedy,
whose signature is the absence of fear, and on Action. On Horror, the genre with the
strongest signature, it finds half of the films but predicts the genre for too many
others. The reason is that the random forest of Stage~1 underestimates the fear of Horror
clips. Adventure, the one genre without a signature, is predicted poorly for how often it
occurs. Both the successes and the errors of the pipeline come with an explanation that can
be checked against how film music is written.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:disc_limitations}

\paragraph{Dataset size.} The Eerola dataset contains 360 rated clips from 45 films. Of
these, 42 films have a modelled genre (40 in the five-genre subset). Comedy and Horror have
only 4 and 5 films. Their film-level $F_1$ therefore moves a lot when a single film
changes, and the bootstrap error is of the same order as the effects being measured. For
the borderline clip-level comparisons, the Nadeau--Bengio test could not become
significant with any number of repeats. Only more films could settle them. The learning
curves were still rising at the full dataset size.

\paragraph{Unit of evaluation.} Scoring per film was adopted after the clip-level analysis
had been done. At that point the analysis of alternative metrics showed that it fits the
structure of the labels. A metric chosen after the results are known can favour the method
it is chosen for. The clip-level score of the same predictions is therefore reported next
to it. The direction of every main conclusion is the same under both. Which comparisons
reach significance differs. Over clips the film bootstrap finds the in-domain lead
significant against VGGish, CLAP, MusiCNN and the PCA-8 control; over films it does not.
For AST it is the other way round. Film-level scoring therefore does not favour the
emotion pipeline. For most comparisons it is the stricter of the two. The reverse
direction of transfer is not significant under either, once films are resampled.

\paragraph{Tests and operating point.} At film level only the film bootstrap can be
computed. Over clips, the stricter Nadeau--Bengio test finds the in-domain advantage
borderline. Both are reported because they measure different sources of uncertainty. All
results are obtained at the default decision threshold, where the advantage is clearest.
With thresholds per genre the pipelines come together in-domain, and the transfer results
were not tested with tuned thresholds.

\paragraph{Choice of the five-genre subset.} The five headline genres were chosen partly
because they carry an emotional signature in the same data. This can favour the emotion
pipeline. The eight-genre results reduce this risk but do not remove it. There the
advantage over VGGish and AST is of similar size and significant at film level.

\paragraph{Labels and listeners.} Genre labels come from IMDb and describe the film, not
the single clip. Some clips therefore carry labels that do not describe their music. IMDb
lists genres alphabetically, so there is no primary genre that could weight the labels. The
emotion model learns perceived emotion from the ratings of one group of listeners.
Listeners from a different musical culture might rate the same clips differently.

\paragraph{The second dataset.} The Blockbuster dataset shares neither audio nor emotion
ratings. The transfer therefore rests on a single representation (VGGish), and the
Blockbuster emotions are checked only indirectly. No film occurs in both datasets, but some
composers do, so stylistic overlap across the split cannot be ruled out. The zero-shot
comparison is a single split. Its point estimates move by a few hundredths with legitimate
choices in the implementation, so it rests on its confidence intervals.

\section{Relation to Prior Work}
\label{sec:disc_relatedwork}

The comparison with \citet{ma2021computational} is like for like. Their protocol,
reproduced on their published features, reaches 0.620 in-domain, inside their reported
range. Under repeated cross-validation the learned embedding does not beat their full set
of hand-crafted features. This agrees with their own careful wording. It also agrees with
the result on Eerola, where direct representations of very different design score close
to one another. \citet{eerola2011genrespecific} showed that an emotion model trained on one
musical genre works much worse on another. Here the same happens to genre models moved
from one film dataset to the next. What is new is that a model built on emotions loses
less. This suggests that the dependence on the
repertoire lies more in the mapping from audio to its representation than in the link
between emotion and genre. \citet{austin2010characterization} found intense genres easier
to separate by their scores than story-driven genres, and the signatures found here
explain why. \citet{mangolin2020multimodal} found audio to be the weakest of five kinds of
input for genre, and the modest absolute scores reported here confirm it. The contribution
of this thesis is therefore not a higher number. It is a model whose predictions can be
explained and which loses less when the data change. This answers the concern of
\citet{kang2025there} that accurate emotion-recognition systems reveal little about how
they work: used as a step in between, the emotion model becomes the explanation. Finally,
the metric analysis explains why Hamming loss, the headline metric of
\citet{behrouzi2023multimodal}, is not used here. On these data, a classifier that always predicts
the most frequent genres wins on it outright.
