Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation} reported the results; this chapter interprets them.
Section~\ref{sec:disc_main} puts the main comparison of all settings side by side,

\section{Interpreting the Main Result}
\label{sec:disc_main}

Table~\ref{tab:disc_settings} lists the emotion route, the PCA-8 control and direct audio
(VGGish) for every setting in which all three were run. Every score is film-level
macro-$F_1$, and each row is compared only with the chance floor of its own label space.

In Table~\ref{tab:disc_settings}, $\Delta$ is the emotion route minus direct audio. The
$p$-value is the paired film bootstrap unless marked $^{\dagger}$ (Nadeau--Bengio corrected
$t$-test over repeated folds; for the Eerola in-domain row computed on the clip-level
scores of the same predictions). The zero-shot row is the strict (source-scaler) regime.

The table shows one pattern. The emotion route is clearly ahead of direct audio where the
genre classifier has been trained on the 40 Eerola films, whether it is tested on
unseen Eerola films or on the 110 Blockbuster films. It is level with direct audio where
the classifier has been trained on the Blockbuster films, in-domain there or with both
datasets in training, and ahead without significance when a classifier trained on
Blockbuster is tested on Eerola. The pooled design shows both sides at once: on the Eerola
films of its test folds the emotion route leads (0.528 against 0.410), on the
Blockbuster films direct audio does (0.579 against 0.568). The advantage of the emotion
route is therefore an advantage under scarce training data and under a change of dataset.
This is a narrower claim than ``emotion predicts genre better than audio'', and the one the
results support.

\paragraph{In-domain.} On the five-genre subset the emotion route is ahead of every direct
representation and of the PCA-8 control, in nearly every repeat and under every seed. Two
qualifications apply. First, the lead is significant only against the weaker direct
representations. Against VGGish it just misses significance at film level ($p=0.052$), and
against the strongest ones and the PCA-8 control it is not significant; the PCA-8 control
is itself ahead of VGGish under every seed. Part of the in-domain gain therefore comes from
compressing the embedding, not from what the eight numbers mean. Second, part of it is an operating-point
effect. The emotion route wins mainly by recall on the rare genres (Section~\ref{sec:eval_pergenre}),
and with a per-genre threshold tuned on the training folds the routes converge
(Appendix~\ref{sec:eval_robustness}). A model that works at the default threshold has a
practical advantage, since per-genre tuning needs labelled data and becomes unreliable for
genres represented by a handful of films, but this advantage is smaller and more specific
than a better ranking of films.

\paragraph{Across datasets.} Trained on Eerola and applied without further fitting to the
Blockbuster films, the emotion route beats direct audio by $+0.106$ ($p=0.013$) and the PCA-8
control by $+0.093$ ($p=0.027$). Its lead is again partly recall-driven. In the reverse
direction it is ahead in both precision (0.407 against 0.362) and recall (0.631
against 0.490), which a shift of the decision threshold alone cannot produce, but the
difference in $F_1$ is not significant over 41 test films. Whether the zero-shot advantage
survives a threshold tuned on the source dataset was not tested (Section~\ref{sec:futurework}).

\section{Why the Bottleneck Helps Where It Does}
\label{sec:disc_bottleneck}

\paragraph{Meaning or compression?} A sceptical reading is that eleven numbers simply
overfit less than 128 on a dataset of about 330 clips. The evidence is mixed. In-domain at
film level, the PCA-8 control gains over the embedding as well, so compression explains
part of the gain, although the emotion route stays ahead of the control under every seed.
Under transfer the picture is clearer in the original zero-shot run, where compressing the
embedding to eight principal components gains $+0.013$ ($p=0.737$) and compressing it to eight
emotions gains $+0.106$; in a second implementation of the same direction the lead over the
control is not significant. The strongest evidence that the emotions carry meaning beyond
their number is the replication of the signatures: the genre profiles derived from human
ratings on Eerola reappear on Blockbuster from predicted emotions ($r=0.836$), and Horror
is defined by fear in both. A principal component has no meaning that could replicate in
this way.

\paragraph{Scarce data, film identity and domain shift.} The direct embeddings contain
strictly more information than the eight emotions predicted from them, yet they do worse
on the small Eerola dataset and across datasets. Three explanations reinforce each other.
With about 40 training films, a linear classifier over 128 to 768 dimensions can fit many
directions that happen to separate the genres of the training films; the emotion stage
adds prior knowledge from a second, independent source of supervision, the listener
ratings. This fits the pattern of Table~\ref{tab:disc_settings}, in which direct audio
catches up exactly where 110 training films are available. A rich embedding also encodes
the sound of individual films (their instrumentation, recording and mixing), which is
useless under film-grouped evaluation, whereas an emotion rating describes what a clip
expresses rather than how it was produced. Finally, an embedding's dimensions carry the
statistics of the recordings they were computed on, while emotion values are expressed on a
rating scale that means the same in any dataset. Once both datasets are in the training
data, the direct classifier can learn both sets of statistics, and its disadvantage
disappears.

\subsection{A low-dimensional genre signal}
\label{subsec:disc_ablation}

The ablation (Appendix~\ref{sec:app_ablation}) shows that no single emotion is necessary
and that one to three emotions keep most of the genre information. The genre signal of the
ratings lies along essentially one threat-versus-warmth axis of fear, tension and valence.
This is one reason why richer representations cannot extract more from the music with the
data available. All eight emotions are retained because the discrete categories make the
model readable: ``Horror is fear'' is a better description of a score than ``Horror is low
valence and high tension'', although a classifier can use either. The same axis explains
why the imperfect emotion predictions work almost as well as the ratings: fear, tension
and valence are among the better-predicted emotions, while the hardest ones, happiness and
sadness, are largely redundant for genre. The ratings are ahead at film level, but not
significantly (0.032, $p=0.277$), so a better emotion model might help a little, but by
much less than its $R^2$ gap suggests.

\subsection{What the bottleneck buys in readability}

Apart from accuracy, the emotion route provides what it was designed for. Each genre the
model predicts well has a readable affective signature. The per-genre results show where
this helps and where it does not. The emotion route gains most on Comedy, whose signature is
the absence of fear, and on Action. On Horror, the genre with the strongest signature, it
finds half of the films but predicts the genre for too many others. Adventure, the one genre
without a signature, is predicted poorly for its frequency. Both the successes and the errors of the pipeline come with an explanation that
can be checked against film-music practice.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:disc_limitations}

\paragraph{Dataset size.} The Eerola dataset contains 360 rated clips from 45 films, of
which 42 films have a modelled genre (40 in the five-genre subset). Comedy and
Horror are represented by 4 and 5 films, so their film-level $F_1$ moves a lot
when a single film changes, and the bootstrap error is of the same order as the effects
being measured. For the borderline clip-level comparisons, the Nadeau--Bengio test could
not become significant with any number of repeats; only more films could settle them. The
learning curves were still rising at the full dataset size.

\paragraph{Unit of evaluation.} Scoring per film was adopted after the clip-level analysis
had been carried out, when the analysis of alternative metrics showed that it fits the
structure of the labels. A metric chosen after the results are known can favour the method
it is chosen for, so the clip-level score of the same predictions is reported alongside.
The direction of every main conclusion is the same under both. Which comparisons reach
significance differs: over clips the film bootstrap finds the in-domain lead significant
against VGGish, CLAP, MusiCNN and the PCA-8 control, over films it does not, and for AST it
is the other way round. Film-level scoring therefore does not favour the emotion route; for
most comparisons it is the stricter of the two. The reverse transfer direction is not
significant under either once films are resampled.

\paragraph{Tests and operating point.} At film level only the film bootstrap can be
computed; over clips, the stricter Nadeau--Bengio test finds the in-domain advantage
borderline. Both are reported because they measure different sources of uncertainty. All
results are obtained at the default decision threshold, where the advantage is clearest;
with per-genre thresholds the routes converge in-domain, and the transfer results were not
tested with tuned thresholds.

\paragraph{Choice of the five-genre subset.} The five headline genres were selected partly
because they carry an emotional signature in the same data, which can favour the emotion
route. The eight-genre results, in which the advantage over VGGish and AST is of similar
size and significant at film level, reduce this risk but do not remove it.

\paragraph{Labels and listeners.} Genre labels come from IMDb and describe the film, not the
individual clip, so some clips carry labels that do not describe the music they contain.
IMDb lists genres alphabetically, so there is no primary genre that could weight the
labels. The emotion model learns perceived emotion from the ratings of one listener
population; listeners from a different musical culture might rate the same clips
differently.

\paragraph{The second dataset.} The Blockbuster dataset distributes neither audio nor
emotion ratings, so the transfer rests on a single representation (VGGish), and the
Blockbuster emotions are validated only indirectly. No film occurs in both datasets, but
some composers do, so stylistic overlap across the split cannot be excluded. The zero-shot
comparison is a single split whose point estimates move by a few hundredths with
legitimate implementation choices, so it rests on its confidence intervals.

\section{Relation to Prior Work}
\label{sec:disc_relatedwork}

The comparison with \citet{ma2021computational} is like-for-like. Their protocol,
reproduced on their published features, reaches 0.620 in-domain, inside their reported
range. Under repeated cross-validation the learned embedding does not beat their full set
of hand-crafted features, which agrees with their own cautious wording and with the
result on Eerola, where direct representations of very different design score close to one
another. \citet{eerola2011genrespecific} found that models of musical emotion lose much of
their accuracy when carried from one musical genre to another; this thesis observes the
same degradation for genre models carried from one film dataset to another, and adds that
the loss is smaller for a model built on emotions. This suggests that the repertoire
dependence lies more in the mapping from audio to its representation than in the
relationship between emotion and genre. \citet{austin2010characterization} found
high-intensity genres more separable by their scores than narrative genres, which the
signatures found here explain, and \citet{mangolin2020multimodal} found audio to be the
weakest of five modalities for genre, which the modest absolute scores reported here
confirm. The contribution of this thesis is therefore not a higher number but a model whose
predictions can be explained and which degrades less when the data change. This answers
the concern of \citet{kang2025there} that accurate emotion-recognition systems reveal little
about their mechanisms: used as an intermediate, the emotion model becomes the explanation.
Finally, the metric analysis supports the decision not to follow
\citet{behrouzi2023multimodal} in using Hamming loss as a headline metric, because on these
data a classifier that always predicts the most frequent genres wins on it outright.
