\subsection{Why the Nadeau--Bengio Test Stays Borderline}
\label{subsec:statistical_power}

The film bootstrap resamples the test films with the trained models held fixed. It asks
how much a difference depends on which films make up the dataset. The Nadeau--Bengio test
\citep{nadeau2003inference} also counts the variation that comes from which films were
used for training. It is therefore the more conservative test. Only the $1/n$ term of its
corrected variance shrinks as repeats are added. For a given dataset, the attainable
$p$-value therefore has a floor, $p_{\lim}$. For every borderline clip-level comparison of
the emotion pipeline, that floor lies above 0.05 (0.069 to 0.200). More repeats could
therefore never make these comparisons significant under this test; only more films could.

In Figure~\ref{fig:metric_family}, each bar is a pipeline's mean score over the ten
repeats minus the random guess of that metric. The whiskers are the standard deviation
over the repeats.

The metrics in the last block reward predicting the frequent genres and hardly care about
the rare ones. Weighted $F_1$ still scores each genre separately. Under every other metric
of this block, the most-frequent baseline, which predicts Drama for every clip, beats every
model. It reaches 0.571 micro-$F_1$ against at most 0.517, an exact-match rate of 0.283
against at most 0.168, and a Hamming loss of 0.218 against at least 0.311. A metric under
which a model that has learned nothing ranks first is not a good headline for a question
about genres such as Horror and Comedy. This is why macro-$F_1$ is used. Among the metrics
that weight genres equally, the emotion pipeline ranks first under both macro-$F_1$ scores
and both threshold-free metrics.

The all-positive classifier fails in the opposite way. Saying yes to every genre gives it a
recall of 1. It reaches 0.430 macro-$F_1$ over clips and 0.434 over films. That puts it
above every audio pipeline at clip level and above every direct one at film level. Its
weighted $F_1$ (0.607) is even the highest in the table. Hamming loss and exact match expose it
(0.691 and 0.000), and so do the threshold-free metrics. Its macro ROC-AUC is exactly
0.500, and its average precision equals the share of clips with each genre. No metric that
needs a threshold catches both trivial classifiers. ROC-AUC catches both, because neither
of them can rank one clip above another.

Hamming loss deserves a closer look. Under it the emotion pipeline is the weakest of the
four audio pipelines (0.406), and it even falls behind the random guess (0.323). The reason
is the number of genres it predicts: 2.35 per clip on average, against 1.54 true genres,
1.85 for VGGish and 1.68 for AST. Hamming loss counts each of the five decisions per clip
equally. A needless positive therefore costs as much as a missed genre. For a rare genre,
the lowest loss comes from never predicting it, which costs only the genre's base rate
(0.085 for Horror). Split by genre, the emotion pipeline makes fewer errors than VGGish on
Action (0.371 against 0.411) and Drama (0.356 against 0.396), and about as many on Crime.
All of the difference is in Comedy (0.438 against 0.286) and Horror (0.403 against
0.235). It predicts these two genres for 148 and 135 of the 329 clips, although only 40 and
28 carry them. This is the operating point driven by recall, described below, seen from the
other side. Macro-$F_1$ rewards finding a rare genre; Hamming loss punishes every attempt.
The human ratings predict even more genres per clip (2.47) and have the highest Hamming
loss of all (0.427). The effect therefore belongs to the emotion features themselves, in
which a tense crime clip resembles a horror clip. It does not come from errors of the
emotion regression.

The decision threshold matters more. When each genre's threshold is chosen to maximise
$F_1$ on the training fold, every pipeline improves. Over clips, the four pipelines then
come together at between 0.420 and 0.433, with the emotion pipeline lowest. It is then no
longer ahead of VGGish ($-0.004$, $p=0.813$) or of the PCA-8 control ($-0.013$,
$p=0.422$). The two threshold-free metrics put it first, but not significantly (macro
average precision $+0.014$, $p=0.352$; macro ROC-AUC $+0.049$, $p=0.063$). Part of the
in-domain advantage at the default threshold therefore comes from the operating point. The
emotion pipeline places more clips of the rare genres above one half (macro recall 0.566
against 0.423 for VGGish, at precision 0.363 against 0.341). The fixed threshold is not
unfair, since the same rule applies to every pipeline. Tuning per genre
\citep{lipton2014optimal} would, however, narrow the gap. Film-level scoring uses the same
cut on averaged probabilities and shares this caveat; thresholds were not tuned per film.
The main transfer experiments use the default threshold as well.
Appendix~\ref{sec:app_zs_threshold} repeats the zero-shot transfer with tuned thresholds.

\subsection{Stability and Reproducibility}

Figure~\ref{fig:stability_repeats} shows how the film-level scores vary over the ten
repeats and over five master seeds.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/stability_repeats.pdf}
  \caption{Stability of film-level macro-$F_1$ on the five-genre subset over repeats and master seeds.}
  \label{fig:stability_repeats}
\end{figure}

In Figure~\ref{fig:stability_repeats}, the coloured dots are the ten repeats under seed
42, summarised by a box plot. The black dashes are the ten-repeat means under each of the
five master seeds (42, 1, 2, 3, 4).

Three sources of variation were kept apart. The first is the ten repeats, each of which
assigns the films to folds differently. Over them, the standard deviation of film-level
macro-$F_1$ is between 0.034 and 0.045. That is 2.0 to 2.5 times the clip-level values of
0.016 to 0.019, because each genre's $F_1$ rests on a few films. The pipelines vary about
equally (the emotion pipeline 0.038, VGGish 0.039, AST 0.045). The second is the sample of
films, where the bootstrap standard error is between 0.027 and 0.038. The third is the
master seed, which changes both the folds and the random forest of Stage~1. Over the
seeds, the ten-repeat mean moves by 0.005 to 0.019. The emotion pipeline stays between
0.460 and 0.481. Its lead is positive under every seed against VGGish ($+0.076$ to
$+0.132$), AST ($+0.142$ to $+0.155$) and the PCA-8 control ($+0.051$ to $+0.071$). The
PCA-8 control is itself ahead of VGGish under every seed ($+0.019$ to $+0.068$). Under
seed 42 the re-run reproduces the per-repeat scores of the main analysis exactly. The
numbers are therefore stable against everything the code controls. What uncertainty is
left is due to the small number of films.

\section{Which Emotions Are Needed?}
\label{sec:app_ablation}

The ablation trains the genre classifier on the eight ground-truth ratings without the
derived features (all eight: 0.493 at film level). It then leaves out each emotion in
turn and tests several subsets motivated by theory (Figure~\ref{fig:ablation}).

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/ablation.pdf}
  \caption{Emotion ablation on the five-genre subset: change in film-level macro-$F_1$ relative to all eight emotions.}
  \label{fig:ablation}
\end{figure}

Each point in Figure~\ref{fig:ablation} is the difference to all eight emotions, with a
95\,\% bootstrap interval. Orange points leave out one emotion; light blue points are
subsets.

No single emotion is necessary. Removing any one changes the score by at most 0.012. One of
the eight changes reaches $p<0.05$: removing tension raises the score by 0.012
($p=0.041$). With eight tests at the 5\,\% level, one such result is roughly what chance
produces. The Nadeau--Bengio test on the clip-level scores does not support it
($p=0.516$), and its direction is the opposite of what a necessary emotion would show.
Smaller subsets lose a little, but not significantly. Fear alone reaches 0.475
($p=0.345$), valence with energy 0.487 and the three dimensional scales 0.479. One to three
emotions thus keep 96 to 99\,\% of the score of all eight. The eight ratings move
together: when tension goes up, fear tends to go up and valence down. In effect the genre
signal runs along a single line, from threat to warmth. Fear is the most telling
emotion, but no emotion is necessary, and all eight are kept because they make the model
easier to read.

\section{The Waveform Representation}
\label{sec:app_waveform}

Used directly, wav2vec~2.0 is the weakest representation for genre (0.261 at film level).
It is behind each of the other five by 0.057 to 0.131. As the input of the emotion
pipeline, with the same random forest and classifier, the same representation reaches
0.472. That is level with the VGGish pipeline and not significantly below the ground-truth
ceiling ($-0.018$, $p=0.546$). The gain over its direct use, $+0.209$ [$+0.137$,
$+0.277$], holds in all ten repeats ($p<0.001$; over clips $+0.086$, Nadeau--Bengio
$p=0.014$). The benefit of the emotion step is therefore not tied to one embedding, and it
is largest where the underlying representation is weakest.

A separate check asked why the representation that predicts the emotions worst gives one
of the strongest emotion pipelines. Its lead over the VGGish emotion pipeline is not
significant ($+0.013$, $p=0.537$, ahead in six of ten repeats; over clips $+0.009$,
Nadeau--Bengio $p=0.438$). It comes from Crime alone ($F_1$ 0.49 against 0.35).
Compression explains part of the gain over direct use. Projecting wav2vec~2.0 onto eight
principal components, the counterpart of the PCA-8 control, lifts it from 0.261 to 0.344
($p=0.004$). The emotion pipeline is a further 0.127 above this control ($p<0.001$, all ten
repeats). A non-linear model alone does not help either: a random forest that classifies
genre from the embedding directly reaches only 0.186.

The low $R^2$ of Stage~1 is measured per clip, whereas genre is scored per film. Averaged
over each film, the predicted valence, tension, fear and anger correlate with the ratings
at 0.80 to 0.85 (VGGish 0.87 to 0.92). Only happiness, sadness and energy are clearly
worse (0.54 to 0.57). The genre signatures computed from these predicted emotions agree
with those of the ratings ($r=0.85$; VGGish 0.92). Used directly, wav2vec~2.0 never
predicts Comedy and hardly ever Horror ($F_1$ 0.00 and 0.07). Its genre information is
spread over 768 dimensions learnt from speech. A linear classifier cannot find it from
about 32 training films. The emotion ratings, in contrast, tell the random forest which
acoustic properties matter. A shortcut through film identity is ruled out. wav2vec~2.0
encodes the film less strongly than VGGish: a clip's five nearest neighbours come from its
own film in 9\,\% of cases, against 15\,\% for VGGish.

\section{All Eight Genres}
\label{sec:app_eight}

On the full eight-genre task (42 films), the emotion pipeline reaches 0.349 [0.295,
0.389] at film level and the ratings 0.381. The most-frequent baseline reaches 0.102 and
a random guess 0.226 (over clips: 0.317 and 0.328). The emotion pipeline is ahead of AST
(0.242) by $+0.107$ ($p<0.001$) and of the VGGish embedding it starts from (0.257) by
$+0.093$ ($p=0.001$), in all ten repeats. Over clips the Nadeau--Bengio test gives
$p=0.022$ against VGGish and $p=0.136$ against AST. The rarest genres are almost never
caught. Documentary, with two films, is never predicted correctly by any pipeline.
Biography (three films) reaches an $F_1$ of 0.11 in the emotion pipeline. Adventure, with
ten films, is predicted poorly for how often it occurs (0.38 against 0.39 for Comedy with
four films). This fits its missing emotional signature (Section~\ref{sec:eval_signatures}).

\label{sec:app_blockbuster}

In Table~\ref{tab:blockbuster_reference}, the published values are quoted from
\citet{ma2021computational}, Table~4. Their models all treat a film as a set of cues.
VGGish and MIR name the features of each cue. \emph{Average pooling} averages the cue
vectors of a film into one vector before classifying it. \emph{Single attention} lets a
small network learn a weight for each cue, so that the more informative cues count more in
that average. \emph{Simple-MI} (simple multiple-instance learning) also summarises each film
by its cues and then classifies it with $k$-nearest neighbours ($k$NN) or a support vector
machine (SVM). The random guess predicts each genre at its base rate, and the plurality label
set gives every film the most common combination of genres. This direct pipeline is within the range of their
VGGish models, and both of their baselines reproduce to within 0.03. The classifier is
thus checked against an outside result before it is used for transfer. Under the repeated
protocol of Figure~\ref{fig:blockbuster_cv}, the learned embedding (0.593) does not beat
the full set of 140 hand-crafted MIR features (0.585; $+0.009$, $p=0.787$). An earlier,
larger gap came from comparing it with the 78 MFCC-family features only (0.516; $+0.077$,
$p=0.062$). The PCA-8 control (0.587) is a serious baseline on this dataset. A random
eight-dimensional projection of the same embedding reaches only 0.487, so the choice of the
eight directions matters.

A Blockbuster film consists of single scored cues (4664 in total, a median of 39 per film,
with a median length of 19\,s), and a cue is the same kind of object as an Eerola clip.
Predicting the emotions of each cue and averaging them afterwards beats predicting them
once from the film-average embedding. In-domain it reaches 0.616 against 0.557 ($+0.059$,
Nadeau--Bengio $p=0.020$, in 92\,\% of the folds). In the zero-shot setting it is only
slightly ahead (0.508 against 0.502). Applying the emotion model to the same unit it was
trained on is thus part of the method. The multi-instance structure itself adds little. A
majority vote over cues is not significantly better than averaging the cues ($+0.028$,
$p=0.388$).

\section{Why Horror Is the Weakest Genre of the Emotion Pipeline}
\label{sec:app_horror}

On the five-genre subset the emotion pipeline reaches a film-level $F_1$ of 0.23 on
Horror. Given the listeners' ratings instead of predicted emotions, the same genre
classifier reaches 0.41 (Figure~\ref{fig:per_genre}). The genre stage is identical, so the
loss arises in the emotion regression. To find it, the eight emotions of every clip were
predicted out of fold exactly as in the pipeline (random forest on VGGish, the $10\times5$
folds grouped by film, averaged over the ten repeats). They were then compared with the
ratings. Table~\ref{tab:horror_fear} gives the mean rated and predicted fear per genre.

\begin{table}[tbp]
  \centering
  \small
  \caption{Rated and predicted fear per genre on the five-genre subset (clip level).}
  \label{tab:horror_fear}
  \begin{tabular}{lrrr}
    \toprule
    Genre & Clips & Rated fear & Predicted fear \\
    \midrule
    Action & 103 & 3.42 & 3.46 \\
    Crime  & 98  & 3.50 & 3.14 \\
    Drama  & 239 & 2.59 & 2.69 \\
    Comedy & 40  & 1.74 & 2.04 \\
    Horror & 28  & 4.35 & 3.73 \\
    \midrule
    All non-Horror clips & 301 & 2.80 & 2.87 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

In Table~\ref{tab:horror_fear}, a clip counts for every genre its film carries, so the rows
overlap. The ratings place Horror clearly above all other genres in fear. The predictions
do not. The predicted fear of the Horror clips (3.73) is only 0.27 above that of the Action
clips, against 0.93 in the ratings. Within the Horror clips, predicted fear explains only
37\,\% of the variance of the rated fear ($R^2=0.37$, against 0.59 over all clips). The
predictions rise by only 0.45 points per point of rated fear. This is regression to the
mean. Only 28 of the 329 clips are Horror, and a random forest predicts averages of training
ratings, so it cannot reach the extreme values of a small group. The rest of the Horror
profile shifts as well. Expressed as Cohen's $d$ against all other clips, fear falls from
$+0.73$ in the ratings to $+0.59$ in the predictions. Energy changes sign, from $-0.15$ to
$+0.38$, so that predicted Horror looks like a tense, energetic action or crime film. The
genre classifier therefore cannot separate Horror from these genres. It predicts Horror
for too many films (precision 0.15) and still misses half of them (recall 0.50). A better
emotion model, or more Horror films to train it on, would be the remedy. The ratings show
that the emotional signal itself is there.

\section{Error Analysis}
\label{sec:app_errors}

The genre classifier uses balanced class weights so that rare genres are not ignored. This
leads it to predict too many genres. On the eight-genre task, under the earlier
single-run protocol, it assigned 3.55 genres per clip on average against 1.87 true ones.
Raising a single decision threshold above 0.5 makes macro-$F_1$ worse, not better, because
it first removes the positive predictions of the rare, distinctive genres. A threshold
tuned per genre is a different matter (Section~\ref{sec:eval_robustness}). A learning
curve trained the models on growing shares of the films. It was still rising at the full
dataset size, both for the emotion features and for the AST embedding. Performance is
therefore limited by the amount of data as well as by the signal. Finally, grouping by
film matters. Under an ungrouped split the AST baseline reaches 0.44 instead of 0.23
(clip-level macro-$F_1$, eight genres, single untuned five-fold run), because it can
recognise the film rather than the genre. The emotion features gain much less from the
same leakage.

\section{The All-Positive Reference}
\label{sec:app_floor}

A classifier that assigns every genre to every film has a recall of 1, and its precision is
the share $p$ of films with the genre. Its $F_1$ is therefore $2p/(1+p)$. On the five-genre
subset its macro-$F_1$ is 0.434 over films and 0.430 over clips.
Table~\ref{tab:app_floor} compares it with the emotion pipeline for each genre.

  \label{tab:app_floor}

    \midrule
    Action & 12 & 0.46 & 0.61 & $+0.147$ & 0.101 & 0.72 \\
    Crime  & 12 & 0.46 & 0.35 & $-0.115$ & 0.103 & 0.47 \\
    Drama  & 29 & 0.84 & 0.76 & $-0.077$ & 0.252 & 0.71 \\
    Comedy & 4  & 0.18 & 0.34 & $+0.146$ & \textbf{0.012} & 0.76 \\
    Horror & 5  & 0.22 & 0.23 & $+0.009$ & 0.916 & 0.61 \\
    \midrule
    Macro  &    & 0.434 & 0.460 & $+0.022$ & 0.518 & 0.655 \\
    \bottomrule

In Table~\ref{tab:app_floor}, $\Delta$ is the emotion pipeline minus the all-positive
classifier, with the $p$-value of the paired film bootstrap. Bold marks $p<0.05$. AUC is the
film-level area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, for which the all-positive
classifier scores 0.50.

The emotion pipeline beats this reference significantly only on Comedy. It is level on Horror
and behind on Crime and Drama, though not significantly. On Crime it ranks the films at
chance (AUC 0.47). Its $F_1$ there comes from assigning Crime to about half of the films,
while 30\,\% carry the genre. Nothing beats always predicting Drama at film level. The
ratings are ahead of the reference by 0.054 ($p=0.086$). Every direct representation is below
it, for example VGGish by 0.071 ($p=0.043$) and MusiCNN by 0.043 ($p=0.269$). Over clips the
emotion pipeline is also below it ($-0.027$, $p=0.218$).

The reason is how often each pipeline says yes. The films carry 1.55 genres on average. The
emotion pipeline assigns 2.27 per film and the ratings 2.21, while the direct representations
assign 1.27 to 1.65. On 40 films, macro-$F_1$ at the default threshold depends heavily on this
rate. A measure without a threshold avoids the problem. The film-level macro ROC-AUC of the
emotion pipeline is 0.655, which is $+0.155$ above the reference ($p<0.001$). Its lead over
direct VGGish on this measure is $+0.071$ ($p=0.093$), over MusiCNN $+0.078$ ($p=0.096$) and
over the PCA-8 control $+0.071$ ($p=0.087$). These leads are about as large as the leads in
$F_1$, so they are not only an effect of the threshold, but they are not significant.

The same reference gives 0.338 on all eight genres (film level), against 0.349 for the emotion
pipeline. On the 110 Blockbuster films it gives 0.438. That is below the in-domain results and
below the zero-shot emotion pipeline (0.508), but above direct VGGish in zero-shot transfer
(0.403).

advantage in the ordering of films remains.

\section{How the Ratings Relate to Each Other}
\label{sec:app_ratings}

Figure~\ref{fig:circumplex} is a schematic. This section shows how the eight rating scales of
the dataset actually relate to each other, over all 360 rated clips. A principal component
analysis of their correlations needs only two components for most of the structure. The first
holds 59.8\,\% of the variance and the second 22.6\,\%, together 82.4\,\%.
Figure~\ref{fig:rating_structure} draws the scales in the plane of these two components.

\begin{figure}[!htb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{figures/rating_structure.pdf}
  \caption{The eight rating scales in the plane of the first two principal components (360 rated clips).}
  \label{fig:rating_structure}
\end{figure}

In Figure~\ref{fig:rating_structure}, each scale is placed by its correlation with the two
components, so a scale close to the circle is described well by this plane. Scales that point
the same way correlate positively, and scales that point in opposite directions correlate
negatively. The arrows are the three dimensional scales, and the grey squares are the five
discrete emotions.

The real picture differs from the schematic in three ways. First, valence and tension are
almost opposite ($r=-0.91$), where the schematic draws them at an angle of 135 degrees. Second,
energy does not lie on the pleasant side. It correlates negatively with valence ($r=-0.40$) and
positively with tension ($r=0.58$), although the schematic draws energy and tension at right
angles. Third, sadness lies on the pleasant side, because it correlates slightly positively with
valence ($r=0.32$). Sad film music in this dataset is thus not rated as unpleasant.

The first component runs from tension, fear and anger to valence and tenderness. This is the
axis from threat to warmth that also carries most of the genre signal
(Appendix~\ref{sec:app_ablation}). Fear and tension are especially close ($r=0.83$), which fits
the finding that one to three emotions keep most of that signal. The second component runs from
sadness to happiness, and energy lies on its upper side. Figure~\ref{fig:circumplex} stays a
schematic of the models from the literature. This section shows what these models look like in
the ratings used here.
