\chapter{Experiments and Evaluation}
\label{ch:evaluation}

This chapter reports the results of the experiments described in Chapter~\ref{ch:methods}
and states for each research question of the introduction which section answers it. RQ1, whether a
soundtrack's emotional profile predicts its film's genre, is answered in
Section~\ref{sec:eval_genre}. RQ2a, which emotions relate to which genres, and RQ3, which
emotions discriminate each genre most strongly, are answered together in
Section~\ref{sec:eval_signatures}. RQ2b, whether routing audio through an emotion
intermediate beats classifying genre directly from audio, is answered twice: within one
dataset in Section~\ref{sec:eval_direct}, and across datasets, where the model is applied
to film music it has never seen, in Section~\ref{sec:eval_transfer}. Two further sections
support these answers. Section~\ref{sec:eval_emotion} first establishes how well emotion
can be predicted from audio at all, since the pipeline depends on it, and
Section~\ref{sec:eval_robustness} checks whether the in-domain conclusions depend on the
choice of metric or on the random seed. The chapter ends with a summary
(Section~\ref{sec:eval_summary}).

Two conventions from Chapter~\ref{ch:methods} apply throughout. Results come from three
label spaces that are never compared directly: the eight Eerola genres (346 clips,
43 films), the five-genre subset (329 clips, 41 films) on which the in-domain comparisons
are made, and the six genres shared with the Blockbuster dataset (319 Eerola clips from
41 films, and 110 Blockbuster films). Every score is therefore given together with the
chance floor of its own label space. In addition, two chance floors are used, as explained
in Section~\ref{sec:fund_evaluation}: the \emph{most-frequent} baseline and the
\emph{base-rate random guess}. The second is always the higher of the two, and the text
says which one it refers to.

\section{Stage 1: Emotion Regression}
\label{sec:eval_emotion}

The first stage of the pipeline predicts the eight emotion ratings from a frozen audio
embedding with a random forest. Its quality is measured by the coefficient of
determination $R^2$, averaged over the eight emotions, under cross-validation grouped by
film on all 360 rated clips; this is the metric by which the six audio representations
are ranked (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation_protocol}). Table~\ref{tab:waveform_stage1}
gives the mean over the eight emotions, and Figure~\ref{fig:stage1_r2} breaks it down by
emotion.

Table~\ref{tab:waveform_stage1} uses the random forest under film-grouped cross-validation
on all $n=360$ clips. Its last column expresses each score as a fraction of the
$R^2 = 0.897$ implied by the agreement between two independent listener panels
(Section~\ref{subsec:rating_reliability}).

Each cell of Figure~\ref{fig:stage1_r2} is the $R^2$ of the random-forest regressor for
one emotion (column) and one audio representation (row), rounded to two decimals; the last
column is the mean over the eight emotions. $R^2=0$ corresponds to always predicting the
mean rating, and the ceiling set by the agreement between two listener panels is $0.897$
on average.

The three general-purpose spectrogram embeddings cannot be told apart: CLAP, AST and
VGGish reach $R^2$ between 0.558 and 0.561. The music-pretrained MusiCNN follows closely
(0.541) but does not lead, so pretraining on music helps less here than might be
expected. What matters is how much emotion-relevant variation a representation exposes
rather than how closely its pretraining data resemble film music. The 103 hand-crafted
descriptors are clearly weaker (0.490). The waveform representation, wav2vec~2.0,
recovers far less of the rating variance than any other (0.323), even at the hidden
layer that was selected for it on this task (Section~\ref{sec:methods_features}), and
its deficit is spread across all eight emotions instead of being concentrated in one. As
discussed in the Methods chapter, this comparison changes the input domain and the
pretraining data (speech instead of general audio or music) at the same time, so the weak result
cannot be blamed on the waveform front-end alone.

The difficulty of the task also differs strongly between emotions
(Figure~\ref{fig:stage1_r2}). For the three strongest representations, tension, energy
and anger are the easiest to predict ($R^2$ between 0.612 and 0.664), valence and fear
follow closely (0.585 to 0.616), and sadness (0.437 to 0.448) and happiness (0.314 to
0.337) are clearly the hardest. This mirrors the ``valence problem'' documented in the
music emotion recognition literature, where arousal is recovered from audio more
reliably than the positive--negative quality of an emotion
\citep{kim2010music,kang2025there}. In this dataset, however, the valence scale itself is
predicted about as well as fear; the difficulty lies with the two discrete emotions that
most directly express positive and negative feeling. Happiness and sadness are the two
hardest emotions for every representation, including the weakest.

\subsection{Reliability of the Ratings and the Attainable Ceiling}
\label{subsec:rating_reliability}

An $R^2$ of 0.56 means little without a reference point, because the ratings that the
regressor is scored against are themselves noisy. The dataset of
\citet{eerola2011comparison} permits a direct estimate of that noise: its second set
re-rates clips drawn from the first with a different listener panel, giving two
independent measurements of the same music on 102 matched clips from 38 soundtracks.

The two panels agree closely. The mean correlation over the eight emotions is $r=0.912$,
and the intraclass correlation for consistency, ICC(C,1), which asks whether the panels
rank the clips alike, is 0.897. Absolute agreement, ICC(A,1), is lower at 0.818, because
on the three bipolar scales (valence, energy and tension) the second panel rated
systematically about 1.25 points higher; on the five discrete emotions the panels differ
by only 0.06 to 0.21 points. The panels therefore agree on what they hear but use the
rating scale differently.

A predictor cannot agree with a noisy target more closely than the target agrees with an
independent measurement of itself, so the largest attainable $R^2$ is about 0.897. The
best representations reach 0.558 to 0.561, about 62\,\% of that ceiling. A value near 1
was therefore never achievable. However, rating noise does not explain the shortfall
either, since more than a third of the attainable variance is still unexplained. The
limit lies in the audio representations and the amount of training data rather than in
the quality of the labels, which is consistent with the range reported for music emotion
recognition in general \citep{kang2025there}.

\section{Genre Classification from Emotion (RQ1)}
\label{sec:eval_genre}

All genre results in this and the next two sections follow the protocol of
Section~\ref{sec:evaluation_protocol}: $10\times5$ cross-validation grouped by film,
nested tuning of $C$, and macro-$F_1$ computed once per repeat on the pooled out-of-fold
predictions of its five test folds, reported as the mean over the ten repeats with a
95\,\% interval from a bootstrap over films. The score is pooled because rare genres are
missing from many single test folds (Comedy, with four films, from 17 of the 50), and a
per-fold macro-$F_1$ scores such a genre as zero there whatever the model predicts. The
earlier per-fold figures for the ratings and the pipeline (0.397 and 0.394, the latter for the earlier in-sample variant of the pipeline) are lower by
0.02 to 0.03 for this reason alone, with the same ranking of the methods.

On the five-genre subset, a classifier trained on the ground-truth emotion ratings
reaches a macro-$F_1$ of 0.428 [0.371, 0.476], and the full pipeline, in which emotions
are predicted from the VGGish embedding, reaches 0.417 [0.365, 0.460]
(Figure~\ref{fig:indomain_eerola5}). Both lie far above the most-frequent baseline of
0.168 and clearly above the base-rate random guess of 0.308. On the full eight-genre task
the corresponding values are 0.321 [0.280, 0.352] and 0.318 [0.279, 0.344], against a
most-frequent baseline of 0.102 and a random-guess floor of about 0.23
(Section~\ref{sec:fund_evaluation}). RQ1 can therefore be answered positively. The
emotional profile of a soundtrack clip predicts its film's genres well above chance,
although far from perfectly.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/indomain_eerola5.pdf}
  \caption{Genre classification on the five-genre Eerola subset: macro-$F_1$ of every route with 95\,\% intervals.}
  \label{fig:indomain_eerola5}
\end{figure}

In Figure~\ref{fig:indomain_eerola5}, each point is the macro-$F_1$ on the pooled
out-of-fold predictions, averaged over the ten repeats of $10\times5$ film-grouped
cross-validation on the 329 clips of 41 films, and the whiskers are 95\,\% intervals from a
bootstrap over films. Green marks the classifier trained on the human emotion ratings
(the ceiling of the emotion route), orange the emotion route with emotions predicted from
VGGish or from wav2vec~2.0, blue an audio representation used directly, and grey the PCA-8
control. The dashed line is the most-frequent baseline (0.168) and the dotted line the
base-rate random guess (0.308).

For the practical use of the pipeline, the most important comparison is the one between
predicted and rated emotions. The difference is $-0.011$ [$-0.035$, $+0.012$], not
significant under either test (film bootstrap $p=0.325$, Nadeau--Bengio $p=0.497$), and
the predicted emotions come out ahead in three of the ten repeats. Emotion values
estimated from audio are thus as informative for genre as the human ratings they were
trained on, so the pipeline needs no emotion annotation at deployment. Training the
genre classifier on out-of-fold rather than in-sample emotion predictions, which is the
methodologically correct choice, makes no measurable difference ($+0.005$ for the
in-sample variant, $p=0.370$).

\subsection{Error analysis}

The kind of errors the classifier makes explains why the score is not higher. The
genre classifier uses class-balanced weights so that rare genres are not simply ignored,
and this leads to over-prediction: on the eight-genre task, under the earlier single-run
protocol, it assigned 3.49 genres per clip on average against 1.87 true ones. The same
pattern holds on the five-genre subset under the current protocol, where the emotion
route has a macro recall of 0.596 but a macro precision of only 0.371
(Section~\ref{sec:eval_robustness}). The individual genres behave very differently. On
the eight-genre task the rarest genres, Biography and Documentary, with fewer than 25
clips each, are almost never caught. Adventure fails in both directions because it has
no emotional signature of its own (Section~\ref{sec:eval_signatures}), so nothing in the
emotion profile separates it from the genres it co-occurs with.

An obvious remedy for over-prediction would be to raise the decision threshold above
0.5. This, however, makes macro-$F_1$ worse. With strong regularisation the predicted
probabilities are compressed towards 0.5, so a single higher threshold does not trim the
least certain predictions evenly across genres; it removes almost all positive
predictions of the rare, distinctive genres first. Horror and Comedy are then hardly
predicted at all, the classifier degenerates towards predicting Drama alone, and
macro-$F_1$, which gives each genre the same weight, falls with it. The over-prediction
is therefore the cost of catching the rare genres at all and is not a calibration error.
A threshold chosen per genre on the training folds is a different matter, and
Section~\ref{sec:eval_robustness} shows that it helps every route.

Finally, a learning curve, in which the models were trained on growing fractions of the
films, was still rising at the full dataset size for both the emotion features and the
AST embedding. Performance is thus limited by the amount of data as well as by the
signal, and a larger dataset would be expected to raise both.

\subsection{Which emotions are needed? (ablation)}

If the eight emotions are an interpretable description of the music, it makes sense to
ask which of them the genre classifier uses. The ablation answers this on the same folds
and with the same metric as the headline, using the ground-truth ratings of the eight
emotions without the three derived features (all eight: 0.426). Each emotion was left
out in turn, and several theory-motivated subsets were tested: the two-dimensional
circumplex of valence and energy, the three dimensional scales, the five discrete
emotions, fear alone, and fear with valence. Figure~\ref{fig:ablation} shows the
difference of each to the full set.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/ablation.pdf}
  \caption{Emotion ablation on the five-genre subset: change in macro-$F_1$ relative to all eight emotions.}
  \label{fig:ablation}
\end{figure}

Each point in Figure~\ref{fig:ablation} is the difference in pooled macro-$F_1$ to the
classifier that uses all eight ground-truth emotions (0.426), on the same $10\times5$
film-grouped folds, with a 95\,\% interval from a bootstrap over films. Orange points leave
out one emotion and light blue points are the theory-motivated subsets; the vertical line
marks no difference. No chance floor is drawn, since the plot shows differences.

The ablation gives a null result. Removing any single emotion changes the score by at
most 0.005 in either direction. Fear alone reaches 0.433, the five discrete emotions
0.431, and valence with energy alone 0.420; none of these differs significantly from the
full set. One of the thirteen comparisons does reach $p<0.05$ under the film bootstrap:
removing tension raises the score by 0.005 ($p=0.010$). With thirteen tests at the
5\,\% level, one nominal hit is roughly what chance alone produces. The stricter
Nadeau--Bengio test does not support it ($p=0.459$), and the direction is the opposite
of what a ``necessary'' emotion would show. The result is therefore not evidence that
tension is harmful; multiple comparisons produce results of this kind.

The interpretation is that the genre signal in the ratings is low-dimensional and
redundant. The eight ratings are strongly intercorrelated (fear rises with tension and
falls with valence), so almost any pair of them recovers the same information. This
confirms the position taken in Section~\ref{subsec:fund_why_not_va}: fear is the most
\emph{diagnostic} emotion, but no emotion is \emph{necessary}, and all eight are retained
for interpretability rather than for predictive necessity. For the next section this
means that the emotion bottleneck could be narrower than eight dimensions without loss,
so comparing it with the PCA-8 control is a conservative choice.

\section{Emotion versus Direct Audio, In-domain (RQ2b)}
\label{sec:eval_direct}

RQ2b asks whether the detour through emotion is better than giving the genre classifier
the audio embedding directly. Figure~\ref{fig:indomain_eerola5} already shows the answer
in outline. All six representations were evaluated on the same folds. Used directly,
VGGish reaches 0.377, AST 0.371, the hand-crafted descriptors 0.363, MusiCNN 0.363, CLAP
0.361 and wav2vec~2.0 0.326. The PCA-8 control, which compresses the VGGish embedding to
eight principal components, reaches 0.372. The emotion route from the same VGGish
embedding reaches 0.417 and is ahead of all of them.

\subsection{Statistical Reliability of the Comparisons}
\label{subsec:statistical_power}

Table~\ref{tab:power_cmp} gives the paired comparisons on the five-genre subset with
both significance tests. The film bootstrap resamples the test films with the trained
models held fixed, and so asks how much a difference depends on which films happen to
make up the dataset. It is also the test used for the zero-shot results of
Section~\ref{sec:eval_transfer}. The Nadeau--Bengio corrected $t$-test
\citep{nadeau2003inference}, applied to the fifty per-fold scores, also charges for the
variation that comes from which films were used for training, and is the more
conservative of the two. Both are reported for every comparison.

In Table~\ref{tab:power_cmp}, $\Delta$ is the difference in pooled macro-$F_1$ with its
95\,\% interval from the film bootstrap, $p_{\text{boot}}$ is the paired film bootstrap,
$p_{\text{NB}}$ the Nadeau--Bengio corrected resampled $t$-test on the per-fold scores, and
$p_{\lim}$ the smallest $p_{\text{NB}}$ this dataset allows with an unlimited number of
repeats. The column ``won'' counts the repeats, out of ten, in which the first method
scores higher; ``predicted emotion'' is the emotion route from VGGish, and bold marks
$p<0.05$.

The ordering is stable. The emotion route scores higher than every direct representation
and than the PCA-8 control in all ten repeats, by 0.040 to 0.055 against the five
spectrogram-based and hand-crafted representations, and the bootstrap interval of every
one of these differences excludes zero.

Among themselves, the direct representations cannot be separated statistically. They
span 0.361 to 0.377, and even AST and CLAP, which differ in architecture and
pretraining, are only 0.008 apart ($p=0.663$). The emotion route is
therefore not being compared with one arbitrarily chosen or poorly suited embedding; it
outperforms general-audio, audio--text, music-specific and hand-crafted features alike.
Compression on its own does not explain the advantage either, because the PCA-8 control
is no better than the VGGish embedding it compresses ($-0.005$, $p=0.771$).

Whether the advantage is significant depends on which of the two tests is used. Under
the film bootstrap every comparison of the emotion route with a direct representation or
with the control is significant ($p$ between 0.017 and 0.042; below 0.001 against
wav2vec~2.0). Under the Nadeau--Bengio test the same comparisons are borderline ($p$
between 0.070 and 0.151), and only the comparison with wav2vec~2.0 is significant. The
effect sizes are identical under both tests; the difference is that the second test also
charges for the choice of training films. For this reason the in-domain advantage is
described here as consistent and of practical size, significant under one test and
borderline under the stricter one. It is not an unqualified significant result. (The
comparison with CLAP that is significant under both tests, $+0.066$ with
$p_{\text{NB}}=0.036$, uses the ground-truth ratings and not the pipeline.)

The remaining uncertainty comes from the dataset rather than from the procedure. Only the
$1/n$ term of the corrected test shrinks as repeats are added, so for a given dataset the
attainable $p$-value has a floor, $p_{\lim}$. For every borderline comparison that floor
lies above 0.05 (0.055 to 0.130), and at ten repeats the test is already within about
0.02 of it. Additional repeats could therefore never make these comparisons significant
under this test; only more films could. This agrees with the learning curve of
Section~\ref{sec:eval_genre}.

On the full eight-genre task the advantage is significant under both tests. The
pipeline reaches 0.318, ahead of the AST embedding (0.276) by $+0.043$
($p_{\text{boot}}=0.012$, $p_{\text{NB}}=0.049$) and of the VGGish embedding it starts
from (0.268) by $+0.051$ ($p_{\text{boot}}<0.001$, $p_{\text{NB}}=0.011$); each of these
differences holds in all ten repeats, against a most-frequent baseline of 0.102. The
ground-truth ratings (0.321) are likewise ahead of AST by $+0.046$
($p_{\text{boot}}=0.008$, $p_{\text{NB}}=0.031$). For comparison, the first version of
this analysis, a single five-fold run with an untuned classifier, produced $p=0.62$ for
the ratings against AST, which illustrates how little one five-fold run can say about a
difference of this size.

Grouping by film matters for this comparison in a second way. Under an ungrouped split
the AST baseline reaches 0.44 instead of 0.26 (eight genres, single untuned five-fold
run), because the classifier can recognise the film rather than the genre
(Section~\ref{sec:fund_evaluation}), while the emotion features gain only about 0.05
from the same leakage. A naive evaluation would therefore have favoured the direct
embedding for the wrong reason, since the emotion features carry far less information
about film identity.

\subsection{The waveform representation}

The waveform representation shows most clearly what the emotion intermediate does. Used
directly, wav2vec~2.0 is the weakest of the six representations (0.326). Its shortfall
against the others is modest and only partly significant: 0.034 to 0.049, significant
under the film bootstrap against VGGish, AST and the hand-crafted descriptors ($p$
between 0.014 and 0.040) but not against CLAP or MusiCNN ($p=0.100$ and $0.096$), and
against none of them under the Nadeau--Bengio test ($p$ between 0.167 and 0.383). The
genre task thus separates representations far less sharply than emotion regression,
where the same six differ by a factor of about 1.7 (Table~\ref{tab:waveform_stage1}).
This supports ranking feature extractors by $R^2$ rather than by genre accuracy.

Routed through predicted emotions, however, the same representation reaches 0.419 [0.367,
0.462] (Figure~\ref{fig:indomain_eerola5}). This is level with the VGGish pipeline, above
every directly used embedding and not distinguishable from the ground-truth ceiling
($-0.009$, $p=0.456$). The gain over its own direct use is $+0.091$ [$+0.049$, $+0.138$],
significant under both tests (film bootstrap $p<0.001$, Nadeau--Bengio $p=0.012$), and
it holds in all ten repeats. The benefit of the emotion intermediate is therefore not
tied to one particular embedding. It is largest where the underlying representation is
weakest, as would be expected from a bottleneck that discards representation-specific
detail and keeps only perceptually meaningful structure.

\section{Robustness: Choice of Metric and Stability}
\label{sec:eval_robustness}

The in-domain conclusions above rest on one metric, macro-$F_1$ at the default decision
threshold of 0.5, and on one random seed. This section checks both: whether macro-$F_1$
is the right metric or a different way of scoring would change the picture, and how
stable and reproducible the cross-validated numbers are. Both checks use the protocol of
the previous sections on the five-genre subset, with the emotion route, the two
strongest direct representations (VGGish and AST), the PCA-8 control, the ground-truth
ratings and the most-frequent baseline. All metrics are computed from the same
out-of-fold predictions, once per repeat. Significance is assessed with the paired film
bootstrap, here with 1000 draws per repeat instead of the 2000 used in
Table~\ref{tab:power_cmp}, so the macro-$F_1$ row can differ from that table in the
third decimal.

\subsection{Is \texorpdfstring{Macro-$F_1$}{Macro-F1} the Right Metric?}

Multi-label classification has no single natural metric \citep{sokolova2009systematic}.
Table~\ref{tab:metric_family} scores every route under a wider family of metrics, and
Figure~\ref{fig:metric_family} shows the most informative of them as the distance of
each route above the base-rate random guess of that metric, so that metrics with
different chance levels can share one axis.

In Table~\ref{tab:metric_family}, ``Random'' is the base-rate random guess, which predicts
each genre independently at its frequency, and ``Most freq.'' is the most-frequent
baseline. $\Delta$ is the emotion route minus direct VGGish with the paired film-bootstrap
$p$-value, and a dash means that it was not computed. For Hamming loss lower is better;
bold marks the best of the four audio routes.

In Figure~\ref{fig:metric_family}, each bar is the mean over the ten repeats of a route's
score minus the base-rate random guess of that metric, so the zero line is the random-guess
floor of every metric; the whiskers are the standard deviation over the ten repeats. The
most-frequent baseline is not drawn. It lies above zero only for micro- and samples-$F_1$
(0.571 and 0.574 against floors of 0.477 and 0.438), where it beats every route.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/metric_family.pdf}
  \caption{The four audio routes under seven metrics, as distance above the random guess (five genres, seed 42).}
  \label{fig:metric_family}
\end{figure}

The table separates into two groups. The metrics in the last block reward predicting the
frequent genres well and hardly care about the rare ones: micro-$F_1$ pools all
clip--genre decisions, samples-$F_1$ and Jaccard score each clip's genre vector, and
exact match and Hamming loss count correct vectors or correct individual decisions.
Under every one of these, the most-frequent baseline, which predicts the most common
genre, Drama, for every clip, beats every model: 0.571 micro-$F_1$ against at most
0.515, an exact-match rate of 0.283 against at most 0.170, and a Hamming loss of 0.218
against at least 0.314. The emotion route even falls below the random guess on exact
match and Hamming loss, because it deliberately predicts more genres per clip. A metric
under which a model that has learned nothing ranks first is not a suitable headline for
a thesis whose question concerns genres such as Horror and Comedy. For this reason
macro-$F_1$ is used. Because it gives every genre the same weight, it cannot be won by
ignoring the rare ones (Section~\ref{sec:fund_evaluation}). Among the
frequency-dominated metrics, weighted $F_1$ is the exception, because it still averages
per-genre $F_1$ values; there the emotion route is first ($+0.048$ over VGGish,
$p=0.004$).

Among the metrics that weight genres equally, the emotion route ranks first under
macro-$F_1$, macro average precision, macro ROC-AUC and film-level macro-$F_1$, and first
or level-first under all of them. Film-level macro-$F_1$ is of particular interest
because genre is a property of the film. When the clip probabilities of each film are
averaged before the decision, the emotion route reaches 0.469 against 0.375 for VGGish
($+0.096$, $p=0.041$), and AST falls to 0.307, barely above its random-guess floor of
0.301. Against the PCA-8 control (0.418) the film-level difference is not significant
($+0.053$, $p=0.322$).

The result for the decision threshold is more important and qualifies the in-domain
finding. Macro-$F_1$ depends on where the predicted probability is cut, and 0.5 is only
a convention. When each genre's threshold is instead chosen to maximise $F_1$ on the
training fold, every route improves and the four routes converge to between 0.427 and
0.441. The direct routes and the control gain most (VGGish from 0.377 to 0.429, AST
from 0.371 to 0.427, PCA-8 from 0.372 to 0.441) and the emotion route least (from 0.417
to 0.440). With tuned thresholds the emotion route is no longer significantly ahead,
neither of VGGish ($+0.011$, $p=0.439$) nor of the PCA-8 control, which now scores level
with it ($-0.001$, $p=0.924$). The two threshold-free metrics, which measure how well a
route ranks clips without cutting anywhere, point in the same direction. The emotion
route is first, but by small margins that are not significant (macro average precision
$+0.011$, $p=0.533$; macro ROC-AUC $+0.041$, $p=0.176$, both against VGGish), and all
routes are only modestly above the random guess on average precision.

Part of the in-domain advantage of the emotion route at the default threshold is
therefore an \emph{operating-point} effect. Its predicted probabilities put many more
clips of the rare genres above 0.5 (its macro recall is 0.596 against 0.439 for VGGish,
at a slightly higher precision), which suits macro-$F_1$. Once every route is given its
own best cut, the direct routes recover most of the gap. The ranking metrics show that
the emotion route orders clips at least as well as any direct route, but they do not
show that, in-domain, it orders them clearly better. The comparison at the default
threshold is not unfair, since the same fixed rule, chosen before any result was seen,
is applied to every route. However, the in-domain claim that can be defended is that the
emotion route loses nothing against direct audio and is ahead at the default operating
point. It is not better under every way of scoring. Per-genre threshold tuning is a
legitimate refinement \citep{lipton2014optimal} that the main protocol does not use, and
it is the natural choice if the only goal is the highest in-domain score.

The zero-shot advantage of Section~\ref{sec:eval_transfer} is a separate result. It
comes from a single split in which the target dataset contributes no labels, so there is
no target data on which to tune a threshold, and a threshold tuned on the source would
itself have to survive the domain shift. All transfer experiments use the default
threshold; whether their margins would survive a tuned threshold was not tested.

\subsection{Stability and Reproducibility}

The second question is whether the numbers would change under a different random
assignment of films to folds, a different sample of films, or a different random seed.
These three sources of variation are examined separately, and
Figure~\ref{fig:stability_repeats} shows the first and the third.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/stability_repeats.pdf}
  \caption{Stability of pooled macro-$F_1$ on the five-genre subset over repeats and master seeds.}
  \label{fig:stability_repeats}
\end{figure}

In Figure~\ref{fig:stability_repeats}, the coloured dots are the ten individual repeats
under seed 42, summarised by a box plot (box: interquartile range with median; whiskers:
the most extreme repeat within 1.5 times the interquartile range). The black dashes to the
right of each box are the ten-repeat means obtained when the whole protocol is re-run under
each of five master seeds (42, 1, 2, 3, 4). Both chance floors, the most-frequent baseline
(0.168) and the base-rate random guess (0.308), lie below the plotted range.

\emph{Variation over splits.} Across the ten repeats, each of which assigns the films to
folds differently, the standard deviation of pooled macro-$F_1$ is between 0.009 and
0.023 depending on the route. The emotion route is among the most stable (0.011, against
0.020 for VGGish and 0.023 for AST), which is visible in Figure~\ref{fig:stability_repeats}
as the tighter cluster of its ten dots (VGGish and AST each have repeats far below the
rest). Individual folds vary much more: the standard deviation over the 50
single-fold scores is between 0.050 and 0.061, because a single test fold contains only
about eight films. For this reason a single five-fold run cannot resolve differences of
a few hundredths, and the protocol is repeated.

\emph{Variation over the sample of films.} The film bootstrap asks how much a score
depends on which 41 films happen to make up the dataset. Its approximate standard error
is between 0.018 and 0.027, larger than the variation over repeats for every route except
AST. This is the dominant source of uncertainty, and only a larger dataset can reduce
it, in agreement with the $p_{\lim}$ analysis of Section~\ref{subsec:statistical_power}.

\emph{Variation over seeds.} The entire $10\times5$ protocol was re-run under four further
master seeds, which changes both the fold assignment and the random forest of Stage~1.
The standard deviation of the ten-repeat mean across the five seeds is between 0.001 and
0.008; for the emotion route, the means lie between 0.410 and 0.417. The difference
between the emotion route and direct VGGish is positive under every seed, between
$+0.037$ and $+0.059$, and so are its differences to AST ($+0.038$ to $+0.047$) and to
the PCA-8 control ($+0.037$ to $+0.046$). The sign of the difference between the PCA-8
control and VGGish, in contrast, changes with the seed ($-0.005$ to $+0.012$), as
expected for a true null under this protocol. The run under seed 42 reproduces the
per-repeat scores of the main analysis exactly for every route.

The cross-validated numbers are therefore stable and reproducible. The position of the
emotion route relative to the other routes does not depend on the seed or on the split,
and the size of the emotion advantage at the default threshold, about 0.04, is several
times the spread of the ten-repeat mean across seeds. The strength of the conclusions is
limited by the number of films rather than by the evaluation procedure, since the
film-sampling error is of the same order as the effect itself.

\section{Emotional Signatures of Genres (RQ2a, RQ3)}
\label{sec:eval_signatures}

RQ2a and RQ3 concern the content of the relationship rather than its predictive
strength: which emotions characterise which genres, and which of them discriminate most
strongly. They are answered with Cohen's $d$ (Section~\ref{sec:fund_evaluation}),
computed for each emotion rating between the clips of a genre and all other clips.
Table~\ref{tab:emotion_genre_signatures} summarises the dominant signature of each of the
eight Eerola genres.

\begin{table}[tbp]
  \centering
  \caption{Dominant emotional signature (Cohen's $d$) of each of the eight Eerola genres.}
  \label{tab:emotion_genre_signatures}
  \begin{tabular}{llr}
    \toprule
    Genre       & Dominant emotion (direction) & Cohen's $d$ \\
    \midrule
    Horror      & fear (high)            & $+0.75$ \\
    Biography   & happiness (high)       & $+0.67$ \\
    Comedy      & fear (low)             & $-0.61$ \\
    Drama       & valence (high)         & $+0.57$ \\
    Documentary & anger (low)            & $-0.51$ \\
    Action      & happiness (low)        & $-0.50$ \\
    Crime       & fear (high)            & $+0.40$ \\
    Adventure   & none with $|d|>0.25$   & -- \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

In Table~\ref{tab:emotion_genre_signatures}, a positive $d$ means that the emotion is
elevated for the genre and a negative $d$ that it is suppressed.

Fear is the strongest single discriminator across the genre set. It separates the
threat-oriented genres (Horror, $d=+0.75$, the strongest genre--emotion association in
the dataset; Crime, $+0.40$) from the light-hearted ones (Comedy, $-0.61$). Valence forms
a second axis, separating the positively toned genres (Drama, $+0.57$; Comedy) from the
negatively toned ones (Horror, $-0.63$; Action). Each of these genres thus has an
interpretable affective fingerprint that agrees with film-scoring convention. Horror is
defined by fear, Comedy by the absence of fear together with positive valence, Drama by
elevated valence, and Action by high tension combined with an absence of happiness. The
signatures of Biography and Documentary are also strong, but they rest on 24 and 17
clips from very few films and should be read with care.

The same analysis shows where the relationship breaks down. Adventure has no distinctive
signature, as no emotion clearly exceeds an effect size of $|d|=0.25$. It co-occurs with several
other genres and shares their affective palette instead of having one of its own. This
explains why, unlike the rare Biography and Documentary, it fails although it is frequent
(Section~\ref{sec:eval_genre}). The uneven strength of the signatures mirrors the
asymmetry reported by \citet{austin2010characterization}: genres with a pronounced,
conventional affective identity are recoverable from the music, while genres defined by
narrative or setting rather than by tone are not. For RQ2a, this means that emotions
relate to genres in a systematic and readable way for most genres. For RQ3, fear is the
most discriminating emotion overall, followed by valence, and each predictable genre is
marked by one or two dominant emotions.

\subsection{Do the Signatures Replicate?}
\label{subsec:signature_replication}

These signatures were derived from human ratings on one dataset. Whether they describe
film music or only that dataset can be tested on the second one. The Eerola-trained
emotion regressor predicts the eight emotions for each of the 4664 Blockbuster cues, and
the same effect sizes are recomputed there for the six shared genres. No emotion
annotation of Blockbuster exists or is used, so any agreement comes from transfer and
not from fitting. Figure~\ref{fig:signatures} shows the two matrices side by side.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/signatures.pdf}
  \caption{Emotional signatures (Cohen's $d$) of the six shared genres on Eerola (left) and Blockbuster (right).}
  \label{fig:signatures}
\end{figure}

Each cell of Figure~\ref{fig:signatures} is Cohen's $d$ of one emotion for one genre
against all other items; red marks an emotion elevated in the genre, blue a suppressed one.
The Eerola matrix is computed from the human ratings of the clips in the shared six-genre
space, the Blockbuster matrix from the emotions predicted for each of the 4664 cues by the
regressor trained on Eerola only. The replication statistics below are computed against the
film-level Blockbuster values, whose magnitudes are larger.

The signatures replicate. Across all 48 genre--emotion cells, the Eerola effect sizes
and the film-level Blockbuster ones correlate at $r=0.836$, and 44 of the 48 signs agree
(92\,\%). Horror is defined by fear in both datasets. Per genre, the correlation is
highest for Romance (0.991), Sci-Fi (0.971), Action (0.965) and Horror (0.949), lower for
Drama (0.805) and lowest for Comedy (0.586). The strongest single emotion agrees for Horror and Sci-Fi.
For Action and Romance it changes within a signature of the same shape (Action is
low-valence, high-anger and low-happiness in both datasets, and only the order of anger
and happiness changes), while for Comedy, the weakest replication, it changes from fear to
happiness.

One caveat concerns magnitude rather than pattern. Measured per film, the Blockbuster
effect sizes are roughly twice the Eerola ones, because averaging some 39 cues per film
shrinks the variation within each group and so inflates $d$. Measured per cue, the unit
comparable to an Eerola clip, they are of the same order (Horror--fear $+0.55$ against
$+0.78$ on Eerola in the shared space). The structure of the emotion--genre relationship
transfers, but the absolute effect size does not. This is the strongest available
evidence that the interpretability claim generalises beyond its own dataset and that the
affective fingerprints belong to film scoring practice rather than to one collection of
films. However, since the Blockbuster emotions are predicted by a model trained on the
Eerola panel, the replication cannot show independence from that panel.

\section{Cross-Dataset Transfer (RQ2b under domain shift)}
\label{sec:eval_transfer}

All results so far are cross-validated within one dataset. That design can establish
that a representation carries genre information, but not that the information is a
property of film music rather than of this particular collection of 43 films. The
experiments in this section therefore train on one dataset and test on the other. They
take place in the six-genre space shared with the Blockbuster dataset of
\citet{ma2021computational} (Section~\ref{subsec:genre_labels}), whose six genres and
labelling rule are taken from that paper, so that both datasets are scored against the
same classes. VGGish is the only representation available on both
(Section~\ref{subsec:methods_blockbuster}); its two versions are numerically compatible,
both being the standard post-processed 128-dimensional embedding, with per-dimension
means correlating at $r=0.97$.

The two datasets differ in how easy they are, and their floors differ accordingly. On
the 110 Blockbuster films, where a genre is predicted per film, the base-rate random
guess reaches 0.292 and the plurality baseline, which always predicts the most frequent
label set, 0.193. On the 319 Eerola clips of the shared space the random guess reaches
0.250, and an in-domain VGGish classifier under film-grouped cross-validation reaches
0.315. Numbers from the two datasets are therefore only ever compared with their floors.

\subsection{Blockbuster in-domain}

A transfer score cannot be interpreted without knowing what in-domain training achieves
on the target, and without first establishing that the classifier used here is
competitive there. The protocol of \citet{ma2021computational} (leave-one-film-out
cross-validation over the 110 films, with macro-averaged precision, recall and $F_1$)
was therefore reproduced with the simple pipeline of this thesis, which uses mean-pooled
VGGish features and binary-relevance logistic regression. It reaches macro-$F_1$ 0.620,
within the range of 0.61 to 0.65 reported for their VGGish models, and both of their
baselines reproduce to within 0.03 (Table~\ref{tab:blockbuster_reference}). The pipeline
is thus validated against an external published result before it is used for transfer.

The same dataset was then evaluated under the repeated protocol used for Eerola: ten
repetitions of five-fold cross-validation over films, with nested tuning of $C$. Films
are independent rows here, so no grouping is needed, but arms that work on individual
cues still split by film. All $p$-values in the rest of this subsection are from the
Nadeau--Bengio test. Two resources that the first analysis had left unused were
included: the full set of 140 hand-crafted MIR features per cue, and the cue structure
itself. Instance majority voting over the cues, the architecture of
\citet{ma2021computational}, reaches 0.621, and the emotion route applied per cue
reaches 0.616. The film-level VGGish embedding reaches 0.593, the PCA-8 control 0.587,
the full MIR set 0.585 and its 78 MFCC-family features 0.516, against a random-guess
floor of 0.295 under this protocol.

These results correct an earlier claim of this work. The learned embedding does not
beat the full hand-crafted feature set on this dataset: VGGish is ahead by only 0.009
($p=0.787$) and wins exactly half of the 50 folds. The earlier, much larger gap came
from comparing VGGish against the MFCC subset only ($+0.077$ under the repeated
protocol, $p=0.062$). This is consistent with \citeauthor{ma2021computational}'s own
results, in which their best MIR model is close to their best VGGish model. In addition,
in-domain on Blockbuster the emotion route is competitive with the direct embedding but
not better. It is nominally ahead of VGGish ($+0.023$, $p=0.536$) and of the PCA-8
control ($+0.029$, $p=0.369$), but neither difference is significant. The PCA-8 control
is a meaningful baseline here, since it beats a random eight-dimensional projection of
the same embedding by 0.100 ($p=0.001$).

\subsection{Zero-Shot Transfer}
\label{sec:zero_shot}

In the zero-shot experiment every model is fitted on the Eerola dataset alone and used
to predict the genres of all 110 Blockbuster films; no Blockbuster label takes part in
training. Where the genre classifier consumes predicted emotions, these are generated
out-of-fold on the training dataset, so that the classifier is fitted on the same kind
of noisy estimates it meets at test time. A single train--test split has no fold
structure, so uncertainty is quantified by a paired bootstrap over the 110 target films
with 2000 resamples. The embedding-based arms need a feature scaler, and two regimes are
possible: keeping the scaler fitted on the source dataset, or re-standardising the
target with its own statistics. All figures below use the first, strict regime, which
is the conservative one because the direct baseline performs better under it.
Figure~\ref{fig:zero_shot} shows the result and Table~\ref{tab:zero_shot} gives all arms
with precision and recall.

Genre information does transfer between the datasets. The best transferred model
reaches 0.511 against a random-guess floor of 0.292, about 82\,\% of the 0.620 obtained
with in-domain training, although each training row is a single clip of about
17 seconds and the test items are whole films. What the models learn is therefore at
least partly a property of film music rather than of the 41 Eerola films in the shared
space.

The central result for RQ2b is that the emotion route transfers better than the
representation it is derived from. It exceeds the direct VGGish classifier by $+0.106$
[$+0.015$, $+0.186$] ($p=0.018$). Measured against each space's own floor, the emotion
route's lead over chance is 1.9 times that of direct VGGish here (0.219 against 0.115
above 0.292), against 1.6 times in-domain on five genres (0.109 against 0.069 above
0.308; Section~\ref{subsec:statistical_power}). Under the alternative scaling regime the
same margin is $+0.116$ ($p<0.001$). This is the setting in which an interpretable
intermediate should help most, because an emotion value denotes the same perceptual
quantity in both datasets. A dimension of an embedding does not, and a classifier
trained on embeddings partly learns the idiosyncratic scaling of its training data. The
scaling ablation is consistent with this reading but does not test it. The emotion arm
receives the same inputs in both regimes by construction, since only the embedding-based
arms are re-standardised (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation_protocol}), whereas the direct
embedding ($0.407 \rightarrow 0.394$) and the PCA-8 control ($0.421 \rightarrow 0.413$)
move with the choice of target statistics.

The advantage is also not simply due to dimensionality reduction. Compressing the same
embedding to eight principal components gains only $+0.014$ over the raw features
($p=0.572$), whereas compressing it to eight emotions gains $+0.106$. The direct
comparison of the two bottlenecks favours emotion by $+0.092$ [$+0.009$, $+0.171$]
($p=0.032$). In-domain the same comparison is $+0.045$ and significant only under the
film bootstrap. In this experiment, then, the gain comes from the emotion bottleneck in
particular and not from compression as such. The subsection on transfer in every direction below qualifies
how robust this particular comparison is.

The emotion route also transfers better than direct audio when the genre classifier is
trained on the human ratings instead of on predicted emotions (0.434), but by less. This
difference was not tested for significance. It suggests that training the classifier on
the same kind of noisy predictions it meets at test time contributes to the transfer.

The drop from in-domain to transferred performance, from 0.620 to 0.511, is consistent
with the music literature. \citet{eerola2011genrespecific} found that models of musical
emotion generalise considerably worse across repertoires than within them, explaining
16\,\% of valence variance across genres against 43\,\% within.

\subsection{Cue Level versus Film Level}

A Blockbuster film consists of individually scored cues (4664 in total, a median of 39
per film, with a median length of 19\,s). A cue is therefore the same kind of object as
an Eerola clip, whereas the average over a film's cues is not. Predicting the emotions
of each cue and averaging the emotions afterwards outperforms averaging the embedding
first and predicting once: 0.511 against 0.482 in the zero-shot setting
(Table~\ref{tab:zero_shot}), and $+0.059$ in-domain on Blockbuster (Nadeau--Bengio
$p=0.020$, in 92\,\% of the folds). At film level the emotion route is still ahead of
the direct embedding under transfer, but the margin is smaller and not significant
($+0.077$, $p=0.066$). Applying the emotion model at the granularity it was trained on
is thus a measurable part of the method. The multi-instance structure itself adds
little, since instance majority voting is not significantly better than averaging the
cues in-domain.

the picture (Figure~\ref{fig:cross_dataset}). In the reverse direction, the genre
classifier is trained on all Blockbuster films and tested on Eerola clips; because only
Eerola has emotion ratings, the emotion regressor is fitted on the Eerola training folds,
and the held-out Eerola fold touches neither fit. In the pooled design both datasets
are in the training set and cross-validation runs over both.

The emotion advantage over the direct embedding holds in both transfer directions. From

0.395 for direct VGGish ($+0.115$, $p=0.016$); from Blockbuster to Eerola, 0.370 against
0.320 ($+0.050$ [$+0.023$, $+0.079$], $p<0.001$), against a random-guess floor of 0.250.
The advantage disappears when both datasets are in the training set. There, the emotion
route (0.432) and the direct embedding (0.428) are level ($+0.004$, Nadeau--Bengio
$p=0.850$), so once the classifier can see examples of the target distribution, direct
features catch up. The advantage of the emotion route is therefore an advantage in
generalisation across datasets. This is the property one would expect from an
interpretable representation whose values mean the same thing in any dataset. The pooled
result also shows where each route is strong: on the Eerola part of the pooled test
folds the emotion route leads (0.476 against 0.422), and on the Blockbuster part the
direct embedding does (0.573 against 0.539).

The comparison with the PCA-8 control is less robust than the one with the direct
embedding. The two implementations of the Eerola-to-Blockbuster direction differ in one
legitimate choice: the zero-shot experiment trains the emotion regressor on the 319
clips of the shared space, the re-implementation on all 360 rated clips. The emotion
route is stable between them (0.511 and 0.508), but the embedding-based arms move by up
to 0.04, the PCA-8 control from 0.421 to 0.465. In the re-implementation the emotion
route is still ahead of the control, but no longer significantly ($+0.045$, $p=0.258$),
and the same holds in the reverse direction ($+0.018$, $p=0.184$) and in the pooled
design ($+0.027$, $p=0.231$). The emotion route is thus ahead of the control in every
transfer design, but significantly so only in the original zero-shot run (in-domain on
Eerola it is significant under the film bootstrap only; Table~\ref{tab:power_cmp}). The
comparison with the direct embedding, in contrast, is significant in both directions and
in both implementations. Single-split numbers move by a few hundredths with such
choices, so the conclusion rests on the bootstrap intervals and not on the third
decimal.

\section{Summary of Findings}
\label{sec:eval_summary}

\emph{RQ1: can a soundtrack's emotional profile predict its film's genre?} Yes, well
above chance but far from perfectly. On the five-genre subset, a classifier on the
human emotion ratings reaches macro-$F_1$ 0.428 and the full pipeline, with emotions
predicted from audio, 0.417, against a most-frequent baseline of 0.168 and a base-rate
random guess of 0.308; on eight genres the values are 0.321 and 0.318 against a
most-frequent baseline of 0.102. Predicted emotions are as useful as the ratings
($-0.011$, $p=0.325$), so the pipeline needs no annotation at deployment. The emotion
regression it rests on reaches about 62\,\% of the $R^2$ that the agreement between two
listener panels allows. The caveat is the size of the dataset: rare genres are
barely learned, and the learning curve was still rising at the full dataset.

\emph{RQ2a and RQ3: which emotions relate to which genres, and which discriminate most?}
Most genres carry a readable affective signature. Fear is the strongest discriminator
(Horror $d=+0.75$, Comedy $-0.61$) and valence the second axis, while Adventure has no
signature at all, which is why it cannot be predicted. The signatures replicate on the
independent Blockbuster dataset from predicted emotions ($r=0.836$ over 48
genre--emotion cells, 92\,\% sign agreement). The caveat here is that fear is the most
diagnostic emotion but not a necessary one, since the ablation shows that almost any
pair of emotions recovers the same genre information.

\emph{RQ2b in-domain: does the emotion intermediate beat direct audio?} Under the
headline metric it is ahead of every spectrogram-based and hand-crafted representation
and of the PCA-8 control by 0.040 to 0.055 on five genres (and of wav2vec~2.0 by 0.089),
in every repeat, and under all five seeds for the three comparisons that were re-run
with different seeds. This is significant under the film bootstrap and borderline under
the stricter Nadeau--Bengio test, whose attainable $p$-value on this dataset lies above
0.05 for these comparisons. On eight genres it is significant under both tests against
AST and VGGish, the only direct representations run there. Routing the weakest
representation, wav2vec~2.0, through emotion raises it by 0.091, significant under both
tests. The robustness analysis qualifies this result, because part of the advantage is
an operating-point effect. With per-genre thresholds tuned on the training folds the
routes converge (0.427 to 0.441) and the difference is no longer significant, and the
threshold-free ranking metrics place the emotion route first only by small,
non-significant margins. In-domain, the emotion route therefore loses nothing against
direct audio and is ahead at the default threshold, but it is not better under every
way of scoring.

\emph{RQ2b under domain shift.} Across datasets the advantage is larger and, against
direct audio, more robust. Trained on Eerola and tested on the 110 Blockbuster films,
the emotion route reaches 0.511 against 0.407 for direct VGGish ($+0.106$, $p=0.018$)
and 0.421 for the PCA-8 control ($+0.092$, $p=0.032$), against a random-guess floor of
0.292 in that label space. The advantage over the direct embedding holds in the reverse
direction as well ($+0.050$, $p<0.001$) and disappears when both datasets are in
training ($+0.004$, $p=0.850$). The advantage over the PCA-8 control is positive in
every design, significant in the original zero-shot run and, in-domain, under the film
bootstrap only. These transfer results use the default threshold on a single split, so
the in-domain threshold finding does not carry over to them directly; whether they would
survive threshold tuning was not tested.

Three limitations recur across these results. The size of the dataset is the first: 41
to 43 films, a learning curve that has not flattened, and a film-sampling error of the
same order as the effects being measured. For the borderline in-domain comparisons, no
amount of resampling could settle the question, only more films. A second limitation is
the metric, since the in-domain advantage is clearest at the default decision threshold
and shrinks when each route is given its best threshold. Finally, the two settings give
different pictures. In-domain the emotion route is consistently but modestly ahead,
while across datasets its lead over direct audio, measured against each space's own
floor, is larger and significant in both directions. The advantage of the interpretable
intermediate is therefore largest on film music from a dataset the model has never
seen, which is where it matters most.
