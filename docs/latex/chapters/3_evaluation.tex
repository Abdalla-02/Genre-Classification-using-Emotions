\chapter{Experiments and Evaluation}
\label{ch:evaluation}

This chapter reports the results of the experiments described in Chapter~\ref{ch:methods}.
It focuses on the results that answer the research questions.
Section~\ref{sec:eval_emotion} first shows how well emotion can be predicted from audio,
since the pipeline depends on it. Section~\ref{sec:eval_genre} answers RQ1: how well the
emotions of a soundtrack predict its film's genre. Section~\ref{sec:eval_direct} answers
RQ3: how the emotion pipeline compares with classifying genre directly from audio within
one dataset. Section~\ref{sec:eval_pergenre} breaks this comparison down by genre.
Section~\ref{sec:eval_signatures} answers RQ2: which emotions characterise each genre, and
how far these signatures hold on the second dataset. Section~\ref{sec:eval_transfer}
answers RQ4: how well the pipelines generalise to film music the model has never seen.
Some analyses check these results without changing them. They cover the choice of metric,
stability over seeds, the emotion ablation, the waveform representation, all eight genres,
the reproduction of the published Blockbuster result and an error analysis, and they are
reported in Appendix~\ref{app:supplementary}.

Two conventions apply throughout. First, every genre score is macro-$F_1$ over films
(Section~\ref{sec:evaluation_protocol}). The predicted probabilities of a film's clips are
averaged, and the film is scored once. Where it matters for the argument, the clip-level
score of the same predictions is given beside it. Second, the results come from three
label spaces that are never compared directly. These are the eight Eerola genres (346
clips, 42 films), the five-genre subset (329 clips, 40 films), on which the in-domain
comparisons are made, and the six genres shared with the Blockbuster dataset (319 Eerola
clips from 40 films, and 110 Blockbuster films). Every score is therefore given with the
chance floor of its own label space. This is either the \emph{most-frequent} baseline or
the higher \emph{base-rate random guess} (Section~\ref{sec:fund_evaluation}).

\section{Stage 1: Emotion Regression}
\label{sec:eval_emotion}

The first stage predicts the eight emotion ratings from a frozen audio embedding with a
random forest regressor of 300 trees (Section~\ref{sec:methods_stage1}). It is scored by
$R^2$, averaged over the eight emotions. The predictions are out-of-fold, from five-fold
cross-validation grouped by film over all 360 rated clips
(Section~\ref{sec:evaluation_protocol}). Table~\ref{tab:waveform_stage1} ranks the six
audio representations and gives the root-mean-square error (RMSE) of the predicted
ratings beside $R^2$. Figure~\ref{fig:stage1_r2} breaks the result down by emotion.
The last column of the table gives each score as a share of $R^2 = 0.897$, the agreement
between two independent listener panels (Section~\ref{subsec:rating_reliability}).

    AST              & log-mel spectrogram & 768 & 0.545 & 1.24 & 61 \\

\end{table}

Each cell of Figure~\ref{fig:stage1_r2} is the $R^2$ of the regressor for one emotion
(column) and one representation (row). The last column is the mean over the eight
emotions. $R^2=0$ means always predicting the mean rating.

The three general-purpose spectrogram embeddings, CLAP, VGGish and AST, cannot be told
apart ($R^2$ between 0.545 and 0.558). MusiCNN, pretrained on music, follows closely
(0.512). The 103 hand-crafted descriptors are clearly weaker (0.476). The waveform model
wav2vec~2.0 recovers far less of the variance in the ratings than any other (0.299). Even
so, as the input of the emotion pipeline it gives a genre result level with VGGish
(Section~\ref{subsec:eval_grid} and Appendix~\ref{sec:app_waveform}). The emotions also
differ in difficulty. For the three strongest representations, tension, energy and anger
are the easiest ($R^2$ 0.608 to 0.661). Valence and fear follow (0.559 to 0.603). Sadness
(0.428 to 0.438) and happiness (0.302 to 0.321) are clearly the hardest. This mirrors the
``valence problem'' of music emotion recognition: audio reveals arousal more reliably than
whether an emotion is positive or negative \citep{kim2010music,kang2025there}.

\subsection{Reliability of the Ratings and the Attainable Ceiling}
\label{subsec:rating_reliability}

The ratings that the regressor is scored against are noisy themselves. The dataset of
\citet{eerola2011comparison} allows a direct estimate. Its second set rates clips from the
first again with a different listener panel. This gives two independent measurements of
the same music on 102 matched clips. The panels agree closely (mean correlation over the
eight emotions $r=0.912$, intraclass correlation for consistency ICC(C,1) $=0.897$). On
the three bipolar scales, though, the second panel rated systematically higher, which
lowers absolute agreement to ICC(A,1) $=0.818$. A predictor cannot agree with a noisy
target more closely than the target agrees with an independent measurement of itself. The
attainable $R^2$ is therefore about 0.897. With the random forest the best representations
reach 62\,\% of it, and with a tuned ridge regression up to 68\,\% (AST,
Appendix~\ref{sec:app_grid}). Noise in the ratings is therefore not what limits the
regressor. The limit lies in the audio representations and the amount of training data.

\section{Genre Classification from Emotion (RQ1)}
\label{sec:eval_genre}

All genre results in this and the next two sections use $10\times5$ cross-validation
grouped by film, with nested tuning of $C$. Each repeat is scored once on its pooled
out-of-fold predictions, per film. The scores are averaged over the ten repeats and come
with a 95\,\% interval from a bootstrap over films.

On the five-genre subset, the logistic-regression genre classifier trained on the human
emotion ratings reaches a film-level macro-$F_1$ of 0.491 [0.411, 0.552]. The full
emotion pipeline reaches 0.460 [0.385, 0.514] (Figure~\ref{fig:indomain_eerola5}). In it,
a random forest predicts the emotions from the VGGish embedding, and the same classifier
maps them to genres. Both lie far above the most-frequent baseline (0.168) and clearly
above the base-rate random guess (0.309). Scored over clips, the same predictions reach
0.416 and 0.405. The answer to RQ1 is therefore that the emotions of a film's soundtrack
predict its genres well above chance, though far from perfectly. The same holds on all
eight genres, where the emotion pipeline reaches 0.349 against a random guess of 0.226
(Appendix~\ref{sec:app_eight}).

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/indomain_eerola5.pdf}
  \caption{Genre classification on the five-genre Eerola subset: film-level macro-$F_1$ of every pipeline with 95\,\% intervals.}
  \label{fig:indomain_eerola5}
\end{figure}

In Figure~\ref{fig:indomain_eerola5}, each filled point is the film-level macro-$F_1$,
averaged over the ten repeats under the master seed 42. The whiskers are 95\,\% intervals
from a bootstrap over films. The hollow marker in each row is the clip-level score of the
same predictions. Every row ends in the same logistic-regression classifier. Green marks
the classifier on the human ratings (the ceiling of the emotion pipeline under this
seed). Orange marks the emotion pipeline, whose random forest predicts the emotions from
the embedding named in brackets. Blue marks an audio representation given to the
classifier directly, and grey the PCA-8 control (VGGish compressed to eight principal
components). The dashed line is the most-frequent baseline and the dotted line the
base-rate random guess.

For using the emotion pipeline, the key comparison is between predicted and rated
emotions. At film level the ratings score 0.032 higher (difference $-0.032$ [$-0.090$,
$+0.021$]). They are ahead in all ten repeats, but the difference is not significant
($p=0.277$). Over clips the two are level ($-0.010$, $p=0.340$). Emotions estimated from
audio therefore carry most of the genre information in the ratings they were trained on.
The pipeline thus needs no emotion labels once it is in use, although a better emotion
model might still gain a little.

\section{Emotion versus Direct Audio, In-domain (RQ3)}
\label{sec:eval_direct}

Figure~\ref{fig:indomain_eerola5} also answers RQ3 within one dataset. Given directly to
the logistic-regression classifier, the six representations reach the following scores:
MusiCNN 0.396, CLAP 0.390, VGGish 0.367, the hand-crafted descriptors 0.365, AST 0.318 and
wav2vec~2.0 0.261. The PCA-8 control, which compresses the VGGish embedding to eight
principal components, reaches 0.390. The emotion pipeline, whose random forest predicts
the emotions from the same VGGish embedding, reaches 0.460. It is ahead of all of them, by
0.065 to 0.196. Table~\ref{tab:power_cmp} gives the comparisons that matter most for the
argument. Appendix~\ref{sec:app_comparisons} lists all paired comparisons.

In Table~\ref{tab:power_cmp}, $\Delta$ is the first pipeline minus the second, with its
95\,\% interval and $p$-value from the paired film bootstrap. ``Won'' counts the ten
repeats in which the first pipeline scores higher. The two columns on the right score the
same predictions over clips with the Nadeau--Bengio corrected $t$-test. That test needs
one score per test fold and therefore cannot be computed per film. Bold marks $p<0.05$.
``Emotion'' is the emotion pipeline on VGGish. The representation it is compared with is
given to the same classifier directly.

Three results stand out. First, the emotion pipeline is ahead of the VGGish embedding it
is computed from by $+0.093$, in 9 of ten repeats. It is ahead of every other direct
representation as well. Whether these leads are significant depends on the comparison and
on the unit of scoring. At film level the film bootstrap finds the lead significant
against AST, the hand-crafted descriptors and wav2vec~2.0. It does not find it
significant against VGGish ($p=0.052$), CLAP ($p=0.156$) and MusiCNN ($p=0.241$). Over
clips the leads are smaller (0.037 to 0.049). There the same bootstrap finds the lead
significant against VGGish, CLAP, MusiCNN, the hand-crafted descriptors and wav2vec~2.0,
but not against AST ($p=0.106$). The stricter Nadeau--Bengio test, whose attainable
$p$-value lies above 0.05 on this dataset, finds it only against wav2vec~2.0
(Appendix~\ref{sec:app_comparisons}).
Second, compression alone explains part of the gain at film level. The PCA-8 control is
ahead of VGGish by $+0.023$ ($p=0.488$, but in 7 of ten repeats and under every seed).
The emotion pipeline's lead over the control, $+0.070$, is not significant ($p=0.089$).
Over clips the control is level with VGGish, and the emotion pipeline is ahead of it
($+0.042$, $p=0.020$). Third, part of the advantage comes from the operating point. With a
decision threshold tuned per genre on the training folds, the pipelines come together over
clips (0.420 to 0.433; Appendix~\ref{sec:eval_robustness}). The answer to RQ3 is
therefore twofold. At the default threshold the emotion pipeline is consistently ahead of
direct audio, in nearly every repeat and under every seed. How large its lead is over the
strongest direct representations, however, cannot be settled on 40 films.

These numbers are stable. Re-running the whole protocol under five master seeds moves the
ten-repeat mean of the emotion pipeline by at most 0.019 (from 0.460 to 0.481). Its lead
over VGGish stays positive under every seed ($+0.076$ to $+0.132$). The main source of
uncertainty is which films the dataset happens to contain (bootstrap standard error 0.027
to 0.038). Only more films could reduce it (Appendix~\ref{sec:eval_robustness}).

\subsection{Other Representations and Emotion Regressors}
\label{subsec:eval_grid}

So far the emotion pipeline has used the VGGish embedding and a random forest, followed by
the logistic-regression classifier. Figure~\ref{fig:emotion_grid} repeats it for each of
the six representations and three emotion regressors. The regressors are the random
forest, ridge regression with its regularisation strength chosen by film-grouped
cross-validation inside the training films, and SVR with an RBF kernel
(Section~\ref{sec:methods_stage1}). Every entry is the complete pipeline, with the same
classifier, folds and scoring as above.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/emotion_pipeline_grid.pdf}
  \caption{The emotion pipeline for every representation and emotion regressor (five genres, 40 films).}
  \label{fig:emotion_grid}
\end{figure}

In Figure~\ref{fig:emotion_grid}, each row of the left panel is one representation. Its
first column is the representation given directly to the logistic-regression classifier.
The other three are the emotion pipeline with each regressor. Each cell gives the
film-level macro-$F_1$, the mean over the ten repeats $\pm$ the standard deviation over
the repeats. Below it is the clip-level score of the same predictions. The darker the
cell, the higher the film-level score. An asterisk marks an emotion pipeline that is
significantly ahead of the direct use of its representation (paired film bootstrap,
$p<0.05$). The line below the panel gives the classifier on the human ratings (the
ceiling under this seed). The VGGish random-forest cell is the emotion pipeline of the
previous sections. The right panel gives the mean $R^2$ of the same regressors in Stage~1
over all 360 rated clips, with five-fold cross-validation grouped by film as in
Section~\ref{sec:eval_emotion}. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:emotion_grid} lists the same
numbers.

Three results follow. First, the emotion pipeline is ahead of the direct use of its
representation in all 18 combinations, by 0.032 to 0.209 at film level and by 0.024 to
0.086 over clips. The gain is largest for AST and wav2vec~2.0, the two weakest direct
representations. There it stays significant for every regressor after correcting for the
18 tests with the Holm method ($p<0.001$). It is smallest for MusiCNN, the strongest
direct representation (0.032 to 0.049, $p\geq0.32$). Second, inside the emotion pipeline
the representation hardly matters. With the random forest, every representation is within
0.034 of VGGish, and none differs from it significantly ($p\geq0.15$). The 18 emotion
pipelines span 0.416 to 0.481, the six direct ones 0.261 to 0.396. Third, the regressor
matters most for the weaker inputs. On the four spectrogram embeddings, ridge regression
and SVR differ from the random forest by at most 0.017, never significantly. On the
hand-crafted descriptors and on wav2vec~2.0 the random forest is best. It is ahead of
ridge regression by 0.044 ($p=0.037$) and 0.046 ($p=0.002$).

A more accurate emotion model does not reliably give a better genre result. Ridge
regression predicts the emotions better than the random forest on VGGish and AST (right
panel of Figure~\ref{fig:emotion_grid}), without a better genre score. On MusiCNN it
predicts them less well and scores higher. On wav2vec~2.0 it has the higher $R^2$ (0.339
against 0.299) but the lower genre score. What the genre classifier needs from Stage~1 is
clearly not the most accurate estimate per clip.

The differences between emotion pipelines are small compared with their spread. The
standard deviation over the repeats is 0.019 to 0.048 for the emotion pipelines and 0.026
to 0.045 for the direct ones. The 95\,\% film-bootstrap interval of every entry is about
0.13 wide. The whole comparison was repeated under four further master seeds, which change
the folds and every model seed. This moves the mean of an entry by at most 0.047. The
emotion pipeline stays ahead of the direct use of its representation in all 90 pairs of
seed and combination, by at least 0.007 (MusiCNN with the random forest). The seeds also
show a pattern the figure does not. On CLAP, ridge regression is ahead of the random
forest under every seed (by 0.010 to 0.039). CLAP with ridge regression is also ahead of
the VGGish pipeline under every seed (0.502 against 0.469 on average,
Appendix~\ref{sec:app_grid}). This combination was picked out after the results were
known and is not tested here. The thesis therefore keeps the VGGish pipeline that was
fixed beforehand.

The answer to RQ3 therefore does not depend on VGGish or on the random forest. The
advantage of the emotion step belongs to the pipeline, not to one embedding.

The emotion pipeline is the best pipeline for Action, Crime and Comedy. For Drama the best
is the hand-crafted MIR features used directly (0.82 against 0.76 for the emotion

40 films, is predicted well by every pipeline, even by the most-frequent baseline. It
therefore separates the pipelines least. The largest difference lies in Comedy (4 films).
Its VGGish probabilities rarely average above one half over a film's clips, so VGGish

(recall 0.78), at a low precision (0.22). Horror (5 films) is the exception. Although fear

precision of 0.41 against 0.36. Its advantage is therefore mainly that it finds the rare
genres, at the price of more false positives. This is the same pattern, driven by recall,
as the operating-point effect of Section~\ref{sec:eval_direct}. Only four or five films
stand behind the two rarest genres. Their $F_1$ values move by about 0.2 when a single
film changes, so the comparison per genre should be read as a description, not as a test.

energy also rises in Horror clips. In the space of predicted emotions, Horror therefore
looks like a tense action or crime film. The classifier assigns it to too many films and
still misses half of the Horror films (Appendix~\ref{sec:app_horror}).

\section{Emotional Signatures of Genres (RQ2)}
\label{sec:eval_signatures}

RQ2 is about what the relationship looks like, not how well it predicts. It is answered
with Cohen's $d$ (Section~\ref{sec:fund_evaluation}), computed for each emotion rating
between the clips of a genre and all other clips.
Table~\ref{tab:emotion_genre_signatures} sums up the strongest signature of each of the
eight Eerola genres. A positive $d$ means that the emotion is raised in the genre.

\begin{table}[tbp]
  \centering
  \caption{Dominant emotional signature (Cohen's $d$) of each of the eight Eerola genres.}
  \label{tab:emotion_genre_signatures}
  \begin{tabular}{llr}
    \toprule
    Genre       & Dominant emotion (direction) & Cohen's $d$ \\
    \midrule
    Horror      & fear (high)            & $+0.75$ \\
    Biography   & happiness (high)       & $+0.67$ \\
    Comedy      & fear (low)             & $-0.61$ \\
    Drama       & valence (high)         & $+0.57$ \\
    Documentary & anger (low)            & $-0.51$ \\
    Action      & happiness (low)        & $-0.50$ \\
    Crime       & fear (high)            & $+0.40$ \\
    Adventure   & none with $|d|>0.25$   & -- \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Fear is the strongest single discriminator. It separates the genres built on threat
(Horror, $d=+0.75$, the strongest link between a genre and an emotion in the dataset;
Crime, $+0.40$) from the light-hearted ones (Comedy, $-0.61$). Valence forms a second
axis. It separates the genres with a positive tone (Drama, $+0.57$; Comedy) from those
with a negative one (Horror, $-0.63$; Action). Each of these genres thus has an emotional
fingerprint that agrees with the conventions of film scoring. Horror is defined by fear,
Comedy by the absence of fear together with positive valence, Drama by raised valence,
and Action by high tension without happiness. The signatures of Biography and Documentary
are also strong, but they rest on very few films. Adventure has no signature: no emotion
exceeds $|d|=0.25$. The genre occurs together with several others and shares their
emotional range. This mirrors the imbalance reported by
\citet{austin2010characterization}. Genres with a conventional emotional identity can be
recovered from the music, while genres defined by story or setting cannot. For the first
part of RQ2, emotions relate to most genres in a systematic way that can be read. Fear is
the most discriminating emotion overall, followed by valence.

\subsection{Do the Signatures Replicate?}
\label{subsec:signature_replication}

The signatures were derived from human ratings on one dataset. The second dataset can
test whether they describe film music in general or only that dataset. The random forest
trained on the VGGish embeddings of the Eerola clips predicts the eight emotions for each
of the 4664 Blockbuster cues. The same effect sizes are then computed there for the six
shared genres. No emotion labels for Blockbuster exist or are used, so any agreement comes
from transfer and not from fitting. Figure~\ref{fig:signatures} shows the two matrices
side by side.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/signatures.pdf}
  \caption{Emotional signatures (Cohen's $d$) of the six shared genres on Eerola (left) and Blockbuster (right).}
  \label{fig:signatures}
\end{figure}

Each cell of Figure~\ref{fig:signatures} is Cohen's $d$ of one emotion for one genre
against all other items. Red marks an emotion raised in the genre, blue a lowered one.
The Eerola matrix is computed from the human ratings, the Blockbuster matrix from the
emotions predicted for each cue.

The signatures replicate. Across all 48 cells of genre and emotion, the Eerola effect
sizes and the film-level Blockbuster ones correlate at $r=0.836$, and 92\,\% of the signs
agree. Horror is defined by fear in both datasets. The replication is closest for Romance,
Sci-Fi, Action and Horror. It is weakest for Comedy, whose strongest emotion changes from
fear to happiness. The absolute effect sizes do not transfer. Averaging some 39 cues per
film inflates $d$, and measured per cue the values are of the same order as on Eerola. The
structure of the link between emotion and genre therefore holds beyond its own dataset,
which answers the second part of RQ2. One caveat remains. The Blockbuster emotions are
predicted by a model trained on the Eerola panel, so the replication cannot show
independence from that panel.

\section{Cross-Dataset Transfer (RQ4)}
\label{sec:eval_transfer}

The results so far are cross-validated within one dataset. They show that a
representation carries genre information. They do not show that this information belongs
to film music in general rather than to one collection of films. The experiments in this
section therefore train on one dataset and test on the other. They use the six-genre space
shared with the Blockbuster dataset of \citet{ma2021computational}
(Section~\ref{subsec:genre_labels}). VGGish is the only representation available on both
(Section~\ref{subsec:methods_blockbuster}), so every pipeline in this section starts from
VGGish. The emotion pipeline predicts the emotions with the random forest of Stage~1, and
every pipeline ends in the logistic-regression classifier of Stage~2. A Blockbuster row is
a film, so every score in this section is a film-level score, as in the in-domain
sections. On the 110 Blockbuster films the base-rate random guess reaches 0.292 and the
plurality baseline 0.193. On the 40 Eerola films of the shared space the random guess
reaches 0.240.

\paragraph{No film occurs in both datasets.} A transfer result would be inflated if the
same film were in the training and the test data. This was checked against the IMDb
identifiers of all films. None of the 42 Eerola films occurs among the 110 Blockbuster
films, and no titles match. The Eerola films all came out before 2011, and the
Blockbuster films between 2014 and 2019. Some composers, however, appear in both datasets.
For example, Danny Elfman scored \emph{Batman} in Eerola and \emph{Justice League} in
Blockbuster, which quotes his Batman theme. Some stylistic similarity across the split is
therefore possible, but it is not a shared film. A related problem was found inside the
Eerola dataset and corrected before the results reported here. One film appeared under
two spellings of its title. Grouping by film therefore treated it as two films and could
place its clips on both sides of a split. All Eerola experiments were re-run with the two
spellings merged. This gives the 42 films (eight genres) and 40 films (five genres) used
throughout.

\subsection{Blockbuster in-domain}

A transfer score needs an in-domain reference on the target. The direct pipeline of this
thesis gives the film-average VGGish embedding to the logistic regression. It first
reproduces the published result on Blockbuster. Under the leave-one-film-out protocol of
\citet{ma2021computational} it reaches macro-$F_1$ 0.620, within the 0.61 to 0.65 they
report for their VGGish models (Appendix~\ref{sec:app_blockbuster}).
Figure~\ref{fig:blockbuster_cv} shows the same dataset under the repeated protocol used
for Eerola ($10\times5$ cross-validation over films).

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/blockbuster_cv.pdf}
  \caption{Blockbuster in-domain: macro-$F_1$ over the 110 films under $10\times5$ cross-validation.}
  \label{fig:blockbuster_cv}
\end{figure}

In Figure~\ref{fig:blockbuster_cv}, each bar is the mean macro-$F_1$ over the 50 test
folds. Error bars show the standard deviation of the ten repeat means. The dotted
line marks the base-rate random guess (0.295). The four pipelines are defined as follows.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Emotion pipeline}: the random forest trained on the 360 rated Eerola clips
    predicts the eight emotions of every cue of a film. The predicted emotions are
    averaged over the film's cues, and a genre classifier trained on the Blockbuster
    training films maps them to genres.
  \item \emph{Direct VGGish, film average}: the VGGish embeddings of a film's cues are
    averaged into one 128-dimensional vector. The genre classifier is trained on these
    vectors directly.
  \item \emph{Direct VGGish, vote over cues}: the classifier is trained on single cues,
    each labelled with its film's genres. A film gets a genre when more than half of its
    cues predict it. This is the instance majority voting of \citet{ma2021computational}.
  \item \emph{PCA-8 control}: the film-average embedding compressed to eight principal
    components, fitted on the training films.
\end{itemize}

In-domain on Blockbuster, the emotion pipeline (0.616 $\pm$ 0.022) is level with direct
audio. It is nominally ahead of the film-average embedding (0.593; $+0.023$,
Nadeau--Bengio $p=0.536$) and slightly behind the vote over cues (0.621). None of these
differences is significant. The PCA-8 control (0.587) is level with them as well.
Appendix~\ref{sec:app_blockbuster} compares the hand-crafted features published with the
dataset.

\subsection{Zero-Shot Transfer}
\label{sec:zero_shot}

In the zero-shot experiment every model is fitted on the Eerola dataset alone. It then
predicts the genres of all 110 Blockbuster films. No Blockbuster label takes part in
training. The emotion classifier is trained on out-of-fold predicted emotions, so that it
sees the same kind of noisy input it meets at test time. A single train--test split has no
folds, so the uncertainty comes from a paired bootstrap over the 110 target films. The
feature scaler is the one fitted on the source dataset (the strict regime). This is the
conservative choice, because the direct baseline scores higher under it.
Figure~\ref{fig:zero_shot} shows the result, and Table~\ref{tab:zero_shot} gives precision
and recall.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/zero_shot.pdf}
  \caption{Zero-shot transfer from Eerola to the 110 Blockbuster films (six shared genres, strict regime).}
  \label{fig:zero_shot}
\end{figure}

In Figure~\ref{fig:zero_shot}, the dashed line is the plurality baseline and the dotted
line the base-rate random guess. The $p$-values are paired bootstraps over the 110 films.

\begin{table}[tbp]
  \centering
  \caption{Zero-shot transfer from Eerola to the 110 Blockbuster films (strict regime).}
  \label{tab:zero_shot}
  \begin{tabular}{lrrr}
    \toprule
    Approach (trained on Eerola only) & Prec. & Rec. & $F_1$ \\
    \midrule
    VGGish $\rightarrow$ random forest $\rightarrow$ emotion (11), per cue & 0.480 & 0.651 & \textbf{0.508} \\
    PCA-8 (VGGish) $\rightarrow$ genre (control)                    & 0.539 & 0.459 & 0.417 \\
    VGGish-128 $\rightarrow$ genre (direct)                         & 0.705 & 0.435 & 0.403 \\
    Random guess                                                    & 0.294 & 0.293 & 0.292 \\
    \midrule
    \emph{In-domain reference (trained on Blockbuster)}             & \emph{0.569} & \emph{0.705} & \emph{0.620} \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Genre information transfers between the datasets. The emotion pipeline reaches 0.508
against a random-guess floor of 0.292. That is about 82\,\% of the 0.620 reached with
in-domain training, although each training row is a clip of about 17 seconds and the test
items are whole films. The central result for RQ4 is that the emotion pipeline transfers
better than the representation it is derived from. It beats direct VGGish (0.403) by
$+0.106$ [$+0.021$, $+0.184$] ($p=0.013$) and the PCA-8 control (0.417) by $+0.093$
[$+0.013$, $+0.171$] ($p=0.027$). Compressing the embedding to eight principal components,
in contrast, gains only $+0.013$ over the raw features ($p=0.737$). Under the other
scaling regime the lead over direct VGGish is $+0.118$ ($p=0.001$). The lead is partly
driven by recall. The emotion pipeline finds more of the true genres (0.651 against
0.435), at lower precision (0.480 against 0.705). In this transfer it makes little
difference whether the emotions are predicted per cue or once from the film-average
embedding (0.508 against 0.502). In-domain on Blockbuster the prediction per cue is
clearly better (Appendix~\ref{sec:app_blockbuster}).

\subsection{Transfer in Every Direction}

Two further designs complete the picture (Figure~\ref{fig:cross_dataset}). In the reverse
direction, the genre classifier is trained on all Blockbuster films and tested on the
Eerola films. The emotion regressor is fitted on the Eerola training folds, and the
held-out Eerola films touch neither fit. In the pooled design both datasets are in the
training set, and cross-validation runs over both.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/cross_dataset.pdf}
  \caption{Film-level macro-$F_1$ for the three cross-dataset designs (six shared genres, VGGish).}
  \label{fig:cross_dataset}
\end{figure}

In Figure~\ref{fig:cross_dataset}, the left group is trained on Eerola and tested on
Blockbuster. The middle group is trained on Blockbuster and tested on Eerola. The right
group is cross-validated with both datasets in training. For the two transfer directions,
the error bars are the bootstrap standard deviation over the test films. For the pooled
design they are the standard deviation of the five repeat means. The left group comes
from a re-implementation in which the emotion regressor is trained on all 360 rated clips,
so it differs slightly from Figure~\ref{fig:zero_shot}.

The emotion pipeline is ahead of direct VGGish in both transfer directions, but
significantly only from Eerola to Blockbuster (0.503 against 0.383, $+0.121$, $p=0.007$).
From Blockbuster to Eerola it reaches 0.458 against 0.364 ($+0.089$ [$-0.017$,
$+0.206$], $p=0.102$), against a random-guess floor of 0.240. Over clips, with whole films
resampled, the reverse difference is $+0.027$ ($p=0.069$). An earlier version of this
analysis resampled clips. Clips of one film are not independent, so that version wrongly
called the difference significant. With both datasets in training, the lead is gone. Per film, the emotion
pipeline (0.531) and direct VGGish (0.543) are level ($-0.011$, Nadeau--Bengio
$p=0.688$). The pooled design also shows where each pipeline is strong. On the Eerola
films of the pooled test folds the emotion pipeline leads (0.528 against 0.410). On the
Blockbuster films direct audio leads (0.579 against 0.568).

The comparison with the PCA-8 control is less robust than the one with direct audio. The
two implementations of the direction from Eerola to Blockbuster differ in one legitimate
choice. The emotion regressor is trained on the 319 clips of the shared space in the
zero-shot experiment and on all 360 rated clips in the re-implementation. The emotion
pipeline is stable between them (0.508 and 0.503). The PCA-8 control, however, moves from
0.417 to 0.457, and the emotion pipeline's lead over it is no longer significant
($+0.049$, $p=0.239$; reverse direction $+0.070$, $p=0.158$). Scores from a single split
move by a few hundredths with such choices. The conclusion therefore rests on the
intervals, not on the third decimal.

\section{Summary of Findings}
\label{sec:eval_summary}

\emph{RQ1.} The emotions expressed by a soundtrack predict its film's genres well above
chance. On the five-genre subset the emotion pipeline (VGGish embedding, random-forest
emotion regressor, logistic-regression genre classifier) reaches a film-level macro-$F_1$
of 0.460, against 0.309 for a random guess. The human ratings reach 0.491. The emotion
regression it rests on reaches about 62\,\% of the $R^2$ that two listener panels allow.

\emph{RQ2.} Most genres carry an emotional signature that can be read. Fear is the
strongest discriminator and valence the second axis. Adventure has no signature. The
signatures replicate on the Blockbuster dataset from predicted emotions ($r=0.836$).

\emph{RQ3.} The emotion pipeline is ahead of every audio representation given directly to
the same classifier (by $+0.093$ over VGGish, its own input). It is also ahead of the PCA-8
control, in nearly every repeat and under every seed. It is the best pipeline for Action,
Crime and Comedy. The lead holds for each of six representations and three emotion
regressors. Through the emotions, the representations end up close together (0.416 to
0.481, against 0.261 to 0.396 when used directly). The lead is significant against the
weaker direct representations. Against the stronger ones and against the PCA-8 control it
is not established. Part of the gain comes from compression, and with per-genre
thresholds the pipelines come together.

\emph{RQ4.} Trained on Eerola and tested on the 110 Blockbuster films, the emotion pipeline
clearly beats direct VGGish (0.508 against 0.403, $p=0.013$). The reverse direction agrees
in sign but is not significant. When both datasets are in training, the pipelines are
level. No film occurs in both datasets.

The main limitation is the size of the Eerola dataset. It has 40 films, two of the five
genres have only four or five films each, and the error from sampling films is of the same
order as the effects being measured. The results also give two different pictures. The
emotion pipeline is well ahead where the genre classifier is trained on the small Eerola
dataset (significantly so in the transfer to Blockbuster). It is level where the
classifier is trained on the larger Blockbuster dataset or on both.
