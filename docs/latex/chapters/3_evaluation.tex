\chapter{Experiments and Evaluation}
\label{ch:evaluation}

This chapter reports the results of the experiments described in Chapter~\ref{ch:methods}.
It follows the pipeline of Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} from start to end.
Section~\ref{sec:eval_emotion} shows how well Stage~1 predicts emotion from audio.
Section~\ref{sec:eval_genre} shows how well Stage~2 predicts genre from emotions.
Section~\ref{sec:eval_direct} compares the whole emotion pipeline with direct audio and with
the PCA-8 control, and Section~\ref{sec:eval_pergenre} breaks this comparison down by
genre. Section~\ref{sec:eval_signatures} looks at which emotions go with which genres.
Section~\ref{sec:eval_transfer} moves to the second dataset and tests transfer between
the two. Section~\ref{sec:eval_summary} sums up the results. Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion}
interprets them and answers the research questions. Further checks that do not change
these results are in Appendix~\ref{app:supplementary}.

Three conventions apply throughout. First, every genre score is macro-$F_1$ over films
(Section~\ref{sec:evaluation_protocol}). The predicted probabilities of a film's clips are
averaged, and the film is scored once. Where it matters, the clip-level score of the same
predictions is given beside it. Second, the results come from three label spaces that are
never compared directly (Table~\ref{tab:label_spaces}). Every score is therefore given with
the chance floor of its own label space: the \emph{most-frequent} baseline or the higher
\emph{base-rate random guess} (Section~\ref{sec:fund_evaluation}). Third, macro-$F_1$ can be
pushed up by saying yes more often. A classifier that assigns every genre to every film
reaches a high macro-$F_1$ but a ROC-AUC of exactly 0.5. Where a lead could come from this,
the threshold-free ROC-AUC is given beside macro-$F_1$
(Section~\ref{subsec:protocol_metrics}).

\section{Stage 1: Emotion from Audio}
\label{sec:eval_emotion}

The first stage predicts the eight emotion ratings from a frozen audio embedding with a
random forest regressor of 300 trees (Section~\ref{sec:methods_stage1}). It is scored by
$R^2$, averaged over the eight emotions, on out-of-fold predictions over all 360 rated clips
(Section~\ref{subsec:protocol_metrics}). Table~\ref{tab:waveform_stage1} ranks the six
audio representations, and Figure~\ref{fig:stage1_r2} breaks the result down by emotion.

\begin{table}[!htb]
  \centering
  \caption{Emotion regression by audio representation: mean $R^2$ over the eight emotions.}
  \label{tab:waveform_stage1}
  \begin{tabular}{llrrrr}
    \toprule
    Representation & Input domain & Dim. & $R^2$ & RMSE & \% of ceiling \\
    \midrule
    CLAP             & log-mel spectrogram & 512 & 0.558 & 1.22 & 62 \\
    VGGish           & log-mel spectrogram & 128 & 0.553 & 1.23 & 62 \\
    AST              & log-mel spectrogram & 768 & 0.545 & 1.24 & 61 \\
    MusiCNN          & log-mel spectrogram & 200 & 0.512 & 1.29 & 57 \\
    Hand-crafted MIR & STFT descriptors    & 103 & 0.476 & 1.33 & 53 \\
    wav2vec 2.0      & raw waveform        & 768 & 0.299 & 1.54 & 33 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

In Table~\ref{tab:waveform_stage1}, RMSE is the root-mean-square error of the predicted
ratings. The last column gives $R^2$ as a share of the ceiling of 0.897 that two
independent listener panels allow (Section~\ref{subsec:rating_reliability}).

\begin{figure}[!htb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/stage1_r2.pdf}
  \caption{Stage~1: $R^2$ per emotion and audio representation (film-grouped cross-validation, 360 rated clips).}
  \label{fig:stage1_r2}
\end{figure}

In Figure~\ref{fig:stage1_r2}, the last column is the mean over the eight emotions.
$R^2=0$ means always predicting the mean rating.

Two results stand out. The three general-purpose spectrogram embeddings, CLAP, VGGish and
AST, cannot be told apart ($R^2$ between 0.545 and 0.558). MusiCNN, pretrained on music,
follows closely (0.512). The 103 hand-crafted descriptors are clearly weaker (0.476), and
the waveform model wav2vec~2.0 recovers far less than any other (0.299). The emotions also
differ in difficulty. For the three strongest representations, tension, energy and anger
are the easiest ($R^2$ 0.608 to 0.661). Valence and fear follow (0.559 to 0.603). Sadness
(0.428 to 0.438) and happiness (0.302 to 0.321) are clearly the hardest.

\subsection{Reliability of the Ratings and the Attainable Ceiling}
\label{subsec:rating_reliability}

The ratings that the regressor is scored against are noisy themselves. The second set of
\citet{eerola2011comparison} rates 102 clips of the first again with a different listener
panel. The panels agree closely (mean correlation over the eight emotions $r=0.912$,
intraclass correlation for consistency ICC(C,1) $=0.897$). On the three bipolar scales,
though, the second panel rated systematically higher, which lowers absolute agreement to
ICC(A,1) $=0.818$. A predictor cannot agree with a noisy target more closely than the target
agrees with an independent measurement of itself. The attainable $R^2$ is therefore about
0.897. With the random forest the best representations reach 62\,\% of it, and with a tuned
ridge regression up to 68\,\% (AST, Appendix~\ref{sec:app_grid}).

\section{Stage 2: Genre from Emotions}
\label{sec:eval_genre}

The second stage predicts genre from the emotions with one logistic regression per genre
(Section~\ref{sec:methods_stage2}). It is tested in two ways. Given the human ratings, it
shows how much genre information the emotions carry at best. Given the emotions that
Stage~1 predicts from the VGGish embedding, it is the full emotion pipeline. All results in
this and the next two sections use $10\times5$ cross-validation grouped by film, with
nested tuning of $C$ (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation_protocol}). The scores are averaged over
the ten repeats and come with a 95\,\% interval from a bootstrap over films.
Figure~\ref{fig:indomain_eerola5} shows every pipeline on the five-genre subset.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/indomain_eerola5.pdf}
  \caption{Genre classification on the five-genre Eerola subset: film-level macro-$F_1$ of every pipeline with 95\,\% intervals.}
  \label{fig:indomain_eerola5}
\end{figure}

In Figure~\ref{fig:indomain_eerola5}, filled points are film-level and hollow markers
clip-level scores, under the master seed 42. Green is the classifier on the human ratings
(the ceiling of the emotion pipeline under this seed), orange the emotion pipeline, blue a
representation given to the classifier directly, and grey the PCA-8 control. The dashed
line is the most-frequent baseline and the dotted line the base-rate random guess.

On the human ratings the classifier reaches a film-level macro-$F_1$ of 0.491 [0.411,
0.552]. The full emotion pipeline reaches 0.460 [0.385, 0.514]. Both lie far above the
most-frequent baseline (0.168) and clearly above the base-rate random guess (0.309). Scored
over clips, the same predictions reach 0.416 and 0.405. On all eight genres the emotion
pipeline reaches 0.349 against a random guess of 0.226 (Appendix~\ref{sec:app_eight}).

Predicted emotions lose little against the ratings. At film level the ratings score 0.032
higher (difference $-0.032$ [$-0.090$, $+0.021$]). They are ahead in all ten repeats, but
the difference is not significant ($p=0.277$). Over clips the two are level ($-0.010$,
$p=0.340$).

\paragraph{A harder reference.} A classifier that assigns every genre to every film has
learned nothing. It still reaches 0.434 on this subset, because macro-$F_1$ rewards recall
and most films are Drama. That is above both chance floors. The emotion pipeline is only
0.022 ahead of it ($p=0.518$), and the ratings 0.054 ($p=0.086$). Over clips both lie below
it (0.405 and 0.416 against 0.430). Every direct representation and the PCA-8 control lie
below it at both levels. The threshold-free macro ROC-AUC separates them clearly. At film
level it is 0.655 for the emotion pipeline, so it orders about two of every three pairs of
a film with and a film without a genre correctly (Section~\ref{sec:fund_evaluation}). It
is 0.691 for the ratings. The classifier that assigns every genre to every film scores
exactly 0.50, and the emotion pipeline's lead of 0.155 over it is significant
($p<0.001$; Appendix~\ref{sec:app_floor}).

\section{The Emotion Pipeline against Direct Audio}
\label{sec:eval_direct}

This experiment tests whether the emotion step helps. Each of the six audio
representations is also given directly to the same logistic regression, without the
emotion step, and the PCA-8 control compresses the VGGish embedding to eight numbers
without any emotional meaning (Section~\ref{sec:methods_baselines},
Table~\ref{tab:pipelines}). All pipelines run on the same folds, so every comparison is
paired. Differences are tested with the paired film bootstrap and, over clips, with the
Nadeau--Bengio corrected $t$-test (Section~\ref{subsec:protocol_metrics}).

Used directly, the six representations reach MusiCNN 0.396, CLAP 0.390, VGGish 0.367, the
hand-crafted descriptors 0.365, AST 0.318 and wav2vec~2.0 0.261
(Figure~\ref{fig:indomain_eerola5}). The PCA-8 control reaches 0.390. The emotion
pipeline, at 0.460, is ahead of all of them, by 0.065 to 0.196.
Table~\ref{tab:power_cmp} gives the most important comparisons, and
Appendix~\ref{sec:app_comparisons} lists all of them.

In Table~\ref{tab:power_cmp}, each row compares the emotion pipeline (VGGish, random forest,
logistic regression) with one other pipeline. $\Delta$ is the emotion pipeline minus the
other, with the 95\,\% interval and $p$-value of the paired film bootstrap. ``Won'' counts
in how many of the ten repeats the emotion pipeline scores higher. The two columns on the
right score the same predictions over clips with the Nadeau--Bengio test. ``Ground truth''
is the same classifier on the human ratings.

Three results stand out. First, the emotion pipeline is ahead of the VGGish embedding it is
computed from by $+0.093$, in 9 of ten repeats, and ahead of every other direct
representation as well. Whether a lead is significant depends on the comparison and on the
unit of scoring. At film level the film bootstrap finds the lead significant against AST,
the hand-crafted descriptors and wav2vec~2.0, but not against VGGish ($p=0.052$), CLAP
($p=0.156$) and MusiCNN ($p=0.241$). Over clips the leads are smaller (0.037 to 0.049).
There the same bootstrap finds the lead significant against VGGish, CLAP, MusiCNN, the
hand-crafted descriptors and wav2vec~2.0, but not against AST ($p=0.106$). The stricter
Nadeau--Bengio test, whose attainable $p$-value lies above 0.05 on this dataset, finds it
only against wav2vec~2.0 (Appendix~\ref{sec:app_comparisons}).

Second, compression alone gives part of the gain at film level. The PCA-8 control is ahead
of VGGish by $+0.023$ ($p=0.488$, but in 7 of ten repeats and under every seed). The
emotion pipeline's lead over the control, $+0.070$, is not significant ($p=0.089$). Over
clips the control is level with VGGish, and the emotion pipeline is ahead of it
($+0.042$, $p=0.020$).

Third, part of the advantage depends on the decision threshold. With a threshold tuned per
genre on the training folds (Section~\ref{subsec:protocol_altmetrics}), the pipelines come
together over clips (0.420 to 0.433; Appendix~\ref{sec:eval_robustness}). Without any
threshold, the emotion pipeline's lead over VGGish in film-level ROC-AUC is $+0.071$
($p=0.093$). That is about as large as its lead in $F_1$, but not significant either.

These numbers are stable. Re-running the whole protocol under five master seeds moves the
ten-repeat mean of the emotion pipeline by at most 0.019 (from 0.460 to 0.481). Its lead
over VGGish stays positive under every seed ($+0.076$ to $+0.132$). The main source of
uncertainty is which films the dataset happens to contain (bootstrap standard error 0.027
to 0.038; Appendix~\ref{sec:eval_robustness}).

\subsection{Other Representations and Emotion Regressors}
\label{subsec:eval_grid}

The emotion pipeline was also run with each of the six representations and three emotion
regressors: the random forest, ridge regression and SVR (Section~\ref{sec:methods_stage1}).
Every entry of Figure~\ref{fig:emotion_grid} is the complete pipeline, with the same
classifier, folds and scoring as above.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/emotion_pipeline_grid.pdf}
  \caption{The emotion pipeline for every representation and emotion regressor (five genres, 40 films).}
  \label{fig:emotion_grid}
\end{figure}

In Figure~\ref{fig:emotion_grid}, the first column of the left panel is the representation
used directly, and the other three are the emotion pipeline with each regressor. Each cell
gives the film-level macro-$F_1$ $\pm$ its standard deviation over the ten repeats, with the
clip-level score below. An asterisk marks a significant lead over the direct use of the same
representation (paired film bootstrap, $p<0.05$). The right panel gives the Stage-1 $R^2$ of
each regressor. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:emotion_grid} lists the same numbers.

Three results stand out. First, the emotion pipeline is ahead of the direct use of its
representation in all 18 combinations, by 0.032 to 0.209 at film level and by 0.024 to
0.086 over clips. The gain is largest for AST and wav2vec~2.0, the two weakest direct
representations. There it stays significant for every regressor after correcting for the
18 tests with the Holm method ($p<0.001$; Section~\ref{sec:fund_evaluation}). It is
smallest for MusiCNN, the strongest direct representation (0.032 to 0.049, $p\geq0.32$).
Second, inside the emotion pipeline the representation hardly matters. With the random
forest, every representation is within 0.034 of VGGish, and none differs from it
significantly ($p\geq0.15$). The 18 emotion pipelines span 0.416 to 0.481, the six direct
ones 0.261 to 0.396. This holds even for wav2vec~2.0, whose emotions are the least accurate
(Appendix~\ref{sec:app_waveform}). Third, the regressor matters most for the weaker inputs.
On the four spectrogram embeddings, ridge regression and SVR differ from the random forest
by at most 0.017, never significantly. On the hand-crafted descriptors and on wav2vec~2.0
the random forest is best, ahead of ridge regression by 0.044 ($p=0.037$) and 0.046
($p=0.002$).

A more accurate emotion model does not reliably give a better genre result. Ridge
regression predicts the emotions better than the random forest on VGGish and AST (right
panel of Figure~\ref{fig:emotion_grid}), without a better genre score. On MusiCNN it
predicts them less well and scores higher. On wav2vec~2.0 it has the higher $R^2$ (0.339
against 0.299) but the lower genre score.

The differences between emotion pipelines are small compared with their spread. The
standard deviation over the repeats is 0.019 to 0.048 for the emotion pipelines and 0.026
to 0.045 for the direct ones. The 95\,\% film-bootstrap interval of every entry is about
0.13 wide. Under four further master seeds the mean of an entry moves by at most 0.047, and
the emotion pipeline stays ahead of the direct use of its representation in all 90 pairs of
seed and combination, by at least 0.007 (MusiCNN with the random forest). On CLAP, ridge
regression is ahead of the random forest under every seed (by 0.010 to 0.039). CLAP with
ridge regression is also ahead of the VGGish pipeline under every seed (0.502 against 0.469
on average). A post-hoc test does not show this lead to be real, however: over all five
seeds it is 0.032 and not significant ($p=0.112$; Appendix~\ref{sec:app_grid}). The thesis
therefore keeps VGGish with the random forest, for the reasons given in
Section~\ref{sec:methods_stage1}.

\section{Emotional Signatures of Genres}
\label{sec:eval_signatures}

This analysis looks at what the link between emotion and genre looks like, not at how well
it predicts. For each emotion rating, Cohen's $d$ compares the clips of a genre with all
other clips (Section~\ref{sec:fund_evaluation}). Table~\ref{tab:emotion_genre_signatures}
gives the strongest signature of each of the eight Eerola genres. A positive $d$ means that
the emotion is raised in the genre.

\begin{table}[!htb]
  \centering
  \caption{Dominant emotional signature (Cohen's $d$) of each of the eight Eerola genres.}
  \label{tab:emotion_genre_signatures}
  \begin{tabular}{llr}
    \toprule
    Genre       & Dominant emotion (direction) & Cohen's $d$ \\
    \midrule
    Horror      & fear (high)            & $+0.75$ \\
    Biography   & happiness (high)       & $+0.67$ \\
    Comedy      & fear (low)             & $-0.61$ \\
    Drama       & valence (high)         & $+0.57$ \\
    Documentary & anger (low)            & $-0.51$ \\
    Action      & happiness (low)        & $-0.50$ \\
    Crime       & fear (high)            & $+0.40$ \\
    Adventure   & none with $|d|>0.25$   & -- \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Fear is the strongest single discriminator. It separates the genres built on threat
(Horror, $d=+0.75$, the strongest link between a genre and an emotion in the dataset;
Crime, $+0.40$) from the light-hearted ones (Comedy, $-0.61$). Valence forms a second
axis. It separates the genres with a positive tone (Drama, $+0.57$; Comedy) from those
with a negative one (Horror, $-0.63$; Action). The signatures of Biography and Documentary
are also strong, but they rest on very few films. Adventure has no signature: no emotion
exceeds $|d|=0.25$.

\subsection{Do the Signatures Replicate?}
\label{subsec:signature_replication}

The second dataset can test whether the signatures describe film music in general or only
the Eerola films. The random forest trained on the VGGish embeddings of the Eerola clips
predicts the eight emotions for each of the 4664 Blockbuster cues. A cue is one continuous
piece of score music as it is used in the film (Section~\ref{subsec:methods_blockbuster}).
The same effect sizes are then computed for the six shared genres. No emotion labels for
Blockbuster exist or are used, so any agreement comes from transfer and not from fitting.

\begin{figure}[!htb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/signatures.pdf}
  \caption{Emotional signatures (Cohen's $d$) of the six shared genres on Eerola (left) and Blockbuster (right).}
  \label{fig:signatures}
\end{figure}

In Figure~\ref{fig:signatures}, red marks an emotion raised in the genre and blue a lowered
one. The Eerola matrix comes from the human ratings, the Blockbuster matrix from the
predicted emotions.

The signatures replicate. Across all 48 cells of genre and emotion, the Eerola effect
sizes and the film-level Blockbuster ones correlate at $r=0.836$, and 92\,\% of the signs
agree. Horror is defined by fear in both datasets. The replication is closest for Romance,
Sci-Fi, Action and Horror. It is weakest for Comedy, whose strongest emotion changes from
fear to happiness. The absolute effect sizes do not transfer. Averaging some 39 cues per
film inflates $d$, and measured per cue the values are of the same order as on Eerola.

\section{Transfer between the Datasets}
\label{sec:eval_transfer}

The results so far are cross-validated within one dataset. The experiments in this section
train on one dataset and test on the other (Section~\ref{subsec:protocol_transfer}). They
use the six genres shared with the Blockbuster dataset of \citet{ma2021computational}
(Section~\ref{subsec:genre_labels}). VGGish is the only representation available on both
datasets, so every pipeline here starts from VGGish. The emotion pipeline predicts the
emotions with the random forest of Stage~1, and every pipeline ends in the same logistic
regression. A Blockbuster row is a film, so every score is a film-level score. On the 110
Blockbuster films the base-rate random guess reaches 0.292 and the plurality baseline 0.193.
On the 40 Eerola films of the shared space the random guess reaches 0.240.

\paragraph{No film occurs in both datasets.} A transfer result would be inflated if the
same film were in the training and the test data. This was checked against the IMDb
identifiers of all films. None of the 42 Eerola films occurs among the 110 Blockbuster
films, and no titles match. The Eerola films all came out before 2011, and the
Blockbuster films between 2014 and 2019. Some composers, however, appear in both datasets.
For example, Hans Zimmer wrote the music for \emph{The Last Samurai} in Eerola and for
\emph{Interstellar} and \emph{Dunkirk} in Blockbuster. Some stylistic similarity across the
split is therefore possible, but it is not a shared film. A related problem was found inside
the Eerola dataset and corrected before the results reported here. One film appeared under
two spellings of its title. Grouping by film therefore treated it as two films and could
place its clips on both sides of a split. All Eerola experiments were re-run with the two
spellings merged. This gives the 42 films (eight genres) and 40 films (five genres) used
throughout.

\subsection{Blockbuster in-domain}

A transfer score needs an in-domain reference on the target. The direct pipeline of this
thesis gives the film-average VGGish embedding to the logistic regression. Under the
leave-one-film-out protocol of \citet{ma2021computational} it reaches macro-$F_1$ 0.620,
within the 0.61 to 0.65 they report for their VGGish models
(Appendix~\ref{sec:app_blockbuster}). Figure~\ref{fig:blockbuster_cv} shows the dataset
under the repeated protocol used for Eerola ($10\times5$ cross-validation over films).

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/blockbuster_cv.pdf}
  \caption{Blockbuster in-domain: macro-$F_1$ over the 110 films under $10\times5$ cross-validation.}
  \label{fig:blockbuster_cv}
\end{figure}

In Figure~\ref{fig:blockbuster_cv}, each bar is the mean macro-$F_1$ over the 50 test
folds. Error bars show the standard deviation of the ten repeat means. The dotted
line marks the base-rate random guess (0.295). The four pipelines are defined as follows.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Emotion pipeline}: the random forest trained on the 360 rated Eerola clips
    predicts the eight emotions of every cue of a film. The predicted emotions are
    averaged over the film's cues, and a genre classifier trained on the Blockbuster
    training films maps them to genres.
  \item \emph{Direct VGGish, film average}: the VGGish embeddings of a film's cues are
    averaged into one 128-dimensional vector. The genre classifier is trained on these
    vectors directly.
  \item \emph{Direct VGGish, vote over cues}: the classifier is trained on single cues,
    each labelled with its film's genres. A film gets a genre when more than half of its
    cues predict it. This is the instance majority voting of \citet{ma2021computational}.
    For example, a film with 40 cues gets Action if at least 21 of them are classified as
    Action.
  \item \emph{PCA-8 control}: the film-average embedding compressed to eight principal
    components, fitted on the training films.
\end{itemize}

In-domain on Blockbuster, the emotion pipeline (0.616 $\pm$ 0.022) is level with direct
audio. It is nominally ahead of the film-average embedding (0.593; $+0.023$,
Nadeau--Bengio $p=0.536$) and slightly behind the vote over cues (0.621). None of these
differences is significant. The PCA-8 control (0.587) is level with them as well.

\subsection{Zero-Shot Transfer}
\label{sec:zero_shot}

In the zero-shot experiment every model is fitted on the Eerola dataset alone and then
predicts the genres of all 110 Blockbuster films. No Blockbuster label takes part in
training. The features of both datasets are standardised with the mean and standard
deviation of the Eerola clips only. This is the strict regime of
Section~\ref{subsec:protocol_transfer}. It uses nothing from the Blockbuster films, and the
direct baseline scores higher under it than under the alternative, so it is the
conservative choice. A single train--test split has no folds, so the uncertainty comes
from a paired bootstrap over the 110 target films. Figure~\ref{fig:zero_shot} shows the
result, and Table~\ref{tab:zero_shot} gives precision and recall.

\begin{figure}[!htb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/zero_shot.pdf}
  \caption{Zero-shot transfer from Eerola to the 110 Blockbuster films (six shared genres, strict regime).}
  \label{fig:zero_shot}
\end{figure}

In Figure~\ref{fig:zero_shot}, the dashed line is the plurality baseline and the dotted
line the base-rate random guess. The error bars are the bootstrap standard deviation over the
110 films, and the $p$-values come from the paired bootstrap over the same films.

  \label{tab:zero_shot}
  \begin{tabular}{lrrr}

    \midrule
    VGGish $\rightarrow$ random forest $\rightarrow$ emotion (11), per cue & 0.480 & 0.651 & \textbf{0.508} \\

Genre information transfers between the datasets. The emotion pipeline reaches 0.508
against a random-guess floor of 0.292. That is about 82\,\% of the 0.620 reached with
in-domain training, although each training row is a clip of about 17 seconds and the test
items are whole films. At the default threshold, the emotion pipeline transfers better
than the representation it is derived from. It beats direct VGGish (0.403) by
$+0.106$ [$+0.021$, $+0.184$] ($p=0.013$) and the PCA-8 control (0.417) by $+0.093$
[$+0.013$, $+0.171$] ($p=0.027$). Compressing the embedding to eight principal components,
in contrast, gains only $+0.013$ over the raw features ($p=0.737$). Under the other
scaling regime the lead over direct VGGish is $+0.118$ ($p=0.001$). The lead is partly
driven by recall. The emotion pipeline finds more of the true genres (0.651 against
0.435), at lower precision (0.480 against 0.705). Macro-$F_1$ is the mean of the $F_1$
values of the genres, not the $F_1$ of the mean precision and recall. Direct VGGish is
precise overall, but its $F_1$ on Romance and Horror is only 0.14 and 0.17. The emotion
pipeline reaches 0.42 and 0.24 on them, and 0.61 instead of 0.38 on Sci-Fi. It makes
little difference whether the emotions are predicted per cue or once from the film-average
embedding (0.508 against 0.502).

\paragraph{Thresholds tuned on the source.} Because the lead is partly driven by recall,
the zero-shot transfer was repeated with a separate threshold per genre. Each threshold was
tuned on out-of-fold probabilities of the Eerola clips, as in-domain
(Section~\ref{subsec:protocol_altmetrics}), and then applied unchanged to Blockbuster. The
$p$-values are again those of the paired bootstrap over the 110 films. The lead over direct
VGGish shrinks from $+0.106$ to $+0.041$ ($p=0.159$), and the lead over the PCA-8 control
from $+0.093$ to $+0.045$ ($p=0.173$). The emotion pipeline hardly moves (0.508 to 0.496),
while direct VGGish rises from 0.403 to 0.455. Tuned on the Eerola films instead of the
clips, direct VGGish is even nominally ahead ($-0.044$, $p=0.246$). Without a threshold,
the emotion pipeline still ranks the Blockbuster films best, with a macro ROC-AUC of 0.763
against 0.720 for direct VGGish ($+0.042$, $p=0.097$) and 0.693 for the PCA-8 control
($+0.070$, $p=0.010$). Appendix~\ref{sec:app_zs_threshold} gives the details.

\subsection{Transfer in Every Direction}

Two further designs complete the picture (Figure~\ref{fig:cross_dataset}). In the reverse
direction, the genre classifier is trained on all Blockbuster films and tested on the
Eerola films. The emotion regressor is fitted on the Eerola training folds, and the
held-out Eerola films touch neither fit. In the pooled design both datasets are in the
training set, and cross-validation runs over both.

\begin{figure}[!htb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/cross_dataset.pdf}
  \caption{Film-level macro-$F_1$ for the three cross-dataset designs (six shared genres, VGGish).}
  \label{fig:cross_dataset}
\end{figure}

In Figure~\ref{fig:cross_dataset}, the error bars are the bootstrap standard deviation over
the test films for the two transfer directions, and the standard deviation of the five
repeat means for the pooled design. The left group is a second implementation of the
zero-shot direction, written to run all three designs with the same code. Its emotion
regressor is trained on all 360 rated clips, because the regressor needs only ratings, not
genre labels, and its film vectors give every cue the same weight. Its scores therefore
differ slightly from Figure~\ref{fig:zero_shot}: 0.503 against 0.508 for the emotion
pipeline and 0.383 against 0.403 for direct VGGish. The zero-shot experiment stays the main
result. It was fixed first, and it carries the further checks: both scaling regimes, the
ratings arm and the tuned thresholds.

The emotion pipeline is ahead of direct VGGish in both transfer directions, but
significantly only from Eerola to Blockbuster (0.503 against 0.383, $+0.121$, $p=0.007$).
From Blockbuster to Eerola it reaches 0.458 against 0.364 ($+0.089$ [$-0.017$,
$+0.206$], $p=0.102$), against a random-guess floor of 0.240. Over clips, with whole films
resampled, the reverse difference is $+0.027$ ($p=0.069$). An earlier version of this
analysis resampled clips. Clips of one film are not independent, so that version wrongly
called the difference significant. With both datasets in training, the lead is gone. Per
film, the emotion pipeline (0.531) and direct VGGish (0.543) are level ($-0.011$,
Nadeau--Bengio $p=0.688$). On the Eerola films of the pooled test folds the emotion
pipeline leads (0.528 against 0.410). On the Blockbuster films direct audio leads (0.579
against 0.568).

The comparison with the PCA-8 control is less robust than the one with direct audio. In the
second implementation the PCA-8 control moves from 0.417 to 0.457, and the emotion
pipeline's lead over it is no longer significant ($+0.049$, $p=0.239$; reverse direction
$+0.070$, $p=0.158$). Scores from a single split move by a few hundredths with such choices.

\section{Summary of Findings}
\label{sec:eval_summary}

\emph{Stage~1.} From the best audio representations the random forest recovers about
62\,\% of the $R^2$ that two listener panels allow. Arousal-like scales are predicted best,
happiness and sadness worst.

\emph{Stage~2.} On the five-genre subset the emotion pipeline (VGGish embedding,
random-forest emotion regressor, logistic-regression genre classifier) reaches a film-level
macro-$F_1$ of 0.460, against 0.309 for a random guess. The human ratings reach 0.491. A
classifier that assigns every genre to every film already reaches 0.434, however. The
threshold-free macro ROC-AUC of 0.655, against 0.50 for that classifier, shows the genre
information more clearly.

\emph{Against direct audio.} The emotion pipeline is ahead of every audio representation
given directly to the same classifier (by $+0.093$ over VGGish, its own input) and of the
PCA-8 control, in nearly every repeat and under every seed. The lead holds for each of six
representations and three emotion regressors. It is significant against the weaker direct
representations, but not against the stronger ones or the PCA-8 control. With per-genre
thresholds the pipelines come together.

\emph{Per genre.} The emotion pipeline is the best pipeline for Action, Crime and Comedy,
mainly through higher recall. It is weakest on Horror.

\emph{Signatures.} Most genres carry an emotional signature. Fear is the strongest
discriminator and valence the second axis. Adventure has no signature. The signatures
replicate on the Blockbuster dataset from predicted emotions ($r=0.836$).

\emph{Transfer.} Trained on Eerola and tested on the 110 Blockbuster films, the emotion
pipeline clearly beats direct VGGish at the default threshold (0.508 against 0.403,
$p=0.013$). With thresholds tuned on the source the lead shrinks to $+0.041$ ($p=0.159$),
although the emotion pipeline still ranks the films best. The reverse direction agrees in
sign but is not significant. When both datasets are in training, the pipelines are level.
No film occurs in both datasets.
