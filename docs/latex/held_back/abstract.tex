Film music is written to express emotions that suit a film's genre, yet computational studies
predict genre from audio without explaining why a soundtrack points to it. This thesis examines
how far the emotions that listeners perceive in a soundtrack reveal a film's genre, and what
changes when genre is predicted through them instead of from the audio. An emotion model is
trained on 360 film-music clips from 45 films, each rated by listeners for eight emotions.
Genre prediction is multi-label and covers the 40 of these films with at least one of five main genres
and the 110 films of the Blockbuster dataset. A random forest predicts the emotions from a VGGish audio embedding, and a logistic regression per genre predicts genre from
them. This emotion pipeline scored 0.460 in film-level macro-averaged $F_1$, against 0.367 for the same
embedding given directly to the classifier, and it ranked films above chance (ROC-AUC 0.655).
This lead at the default threshold was not significant on 40 films, and with thresholds tuned
per genre the two converged. Transferred to the Blockbuster films, it scored 0.508 against 0.403 for direct audio, a gap that shrank and lost significance with
tuned thresholds. Most genres had their own emotional profile, such as fear in Horror. The
results suggest that emotion does not make genre prediction more accurate in general. Its lead
appeared on the small dataset and under transfer at the default threshold, and its main value
is interpretability.
