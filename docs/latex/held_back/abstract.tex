Film music is written to evoke emotions that suit a film's genre. Computational studies, however, predict genre from audio and do not explain why a soundtrack points to
it. This thesis examines how far a soundtrack's emotions reveal a film's genre, and what changes when
genre is predicted through them instead of from the audio. The emotion model is
trained on 360 film-music clips from 45 films, each rated by listeners for eight emotions.
Genre prediction is multi-label, since a film can have several genres. It covers the 40 of these films with one of five main genres, and 110
further films. A random forest predicts the emotions from a pretrained VGGish audio
embedding, and a logistic regression per genre predicts genre from them. This emotion
pipeline scored 0.460 in film-level macro-averaged $F_1$, against 0.491 with human ratings
and 0.309 for random guessing. It led the same embedding used directly in nearly every
repeat and with all six audio representations tested, but not significantly on 40 films.
Trained on the rated films and tested on the 110 new ones, it scored 0.508 against 0.403
for direct audio. With decision thresholds tuned on the training data, the gap shrank to 0.041 and was no longer significant. Most genres had their own emotional profile, such as fear in Horror. The results suggest that emotion does not make
genre prediction more accurate in general, but offers an interpretable representation that helps most when training data are scarce or the data change.
