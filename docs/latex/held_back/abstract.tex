Film music is written to evoke emotions that suit a film's genre. Computational studies,
however, classify film genre directly from audio features without a fixed meaning and offer
no direct explanation of why a soundtrack points to a genre. This thesis examines how well
soundtrack emotions predict film genre, and how an interpretable emotion step compares with
classifying genre directly from audio. I used 360 film-music clips from 45 films, rated by
listeners on eight emotion scales, and an independent dataset of 110 films. A random forest
predicts the eight emotions from a pretrained VGGish audio embedding, and a logistic
regression per genre predicts genre from them. On five genres, this emotion pipeline reached
a film-level macro-averaged $F_1$ score of 0.460, against 0.491 with the human ratings and
0.309 for a random guess. It led the same embedding used directly in nearly every repeat,
and the emotion step helped with all six audio representations tested. On 40 films,
however, this lead was not significant. Trained on the rated films and applied to the 110
new ones, the emotion pipeline transferred better than direct audio (0.508 against 0.403),
although most of this lead depended on the decision threshold. The genres showed emotional
signatures that fit film-scoring conventions, such as fear in Horror, and these largely
replicated on the second dataset. The results suggest that emotion does not make genre
prediction more accurate in general. Its value lies in an interpretable representation that
helps most when training data are scarce or the data change.
