Film music is written to evoke emotions that suit a film's genre. Computational studies,
however, predict genre straight from audio and do not explain why a soundtrack points to
it. I study how far a soundtrack's emotions reveal a film's genre, and what changes when
genre is predicted through them instead of from the audio itself. The data are 360
film-music clips from 45 films, each rated by listeners for eight emotions, and a second
set of 110 films. A random forest predicts the eight emotions from a pretrained VGGish
audio embedding, and a logistic regression per genre predicts genre from them. On five
genres, this emotion pipeline scored 0.460 in film-level macro-averaged $F_1$. Human
ratings in its place scored 0.491, random guessing 0.309. It led the same embedding used
directly in nearly every repeat, and the emotion step helped with all six audio
representations tested. With only 40 films, though, the difference was not significant. A
larger gap appeared when I trained on the rated films and tested on the 110 new ones:
0.508 for the emotion pipeline, 0.403 for direct audio. Much of this gap disappeared once
the decision threshold was tuned. Each genre also had its own emotional profile. Horror,
for instance, was marked by fear, and most profiles held on the second dataset. The
results suggest emotion does not make genre prediction more accurate in general. Its value
lies in an interpretable representation that helps most when training data are scarce or
the data change.
